# The standards are not silent

R.A. Jacob Martone — proposed, source-based continuation of the standards argument.

> **WORKING DRAFT — CLAIM VERIFICATION INCOMPLETE.** This is a candidate passage, not an applied manuscript edit or author-accepted edition. Current source descriptions and the narrator's normative questions remain distinct from findings about particular encounters.

<!-- BEGIN CANDIDATE -->
<!-- Q03-P01 | claims: Q03-C12,Q03-C13 -->
The standards are not silent about the work I am asking medicine to perform. Before another demand is directed at the patient, read what the profession has already directed at itself.

<!-- Q03-P02 | claims: Q03-C01,Q03-C02 -->
The World Medical Association describes the relationship through responsibilities to alleviate suffering and respect the patient's beliefs and autonomy. Its Declaration of Cordoba also addresses administrative and economic interference with that relationship.[1] These are not merely descriptions of how a patient should behave.

<!-- Q03-P03 | claims: Q03-C12,Q03-C13 -->
When I ask who will help me across a barrier, I am asking where those responsibilities have been assigned. A boundary around one professional's role can be legitimate. Leaving the necessary work without an owner is a different decision, and it deserves a different explanation.

<!-- Q03-P04 | claims: Q03-C03,Q03-C04,Q03-C05 -->
The American Medical Association expressly describes physicians as advocates for their patients. Its account of patient rights addresses coordination of medically indicated care, and notice and reasonable help with alternatives when treatment is discontinued.[2] Advocacy is not an activity reserved exclusively for the patient.

<!-- Q03-P05 | claims: Q03-C12,Q03-C13 -->
I do not confuse that clinical advocacy with legal representation. I ask that the distinction protect both responsibilities rather than make the patient responsible for the unassisted management of either. The fact that my doctor is not my lawyer does not answer who is following the clinical question.

<!-- Q03-P06 | claims: Q03-C06,Q03-C07,Q03-C08 -->
The Department of Justice's mobility-access guidance says providers cannot require patients to bring someone to help at an examination. It addresses reasonable assistance, communication with the patient rather than automatically with the companion, and trained staff and equipment that protect staff while making services accessible.[3]

<!-- Q03-P07 | claims: Q03-C12,Q03-C13 -->
There is a difference between asking my family how best to help me and making my family the condition under which help exists. My participation can guide the work. It should not become the resource that conceals the absence of a workable arrangement.

<!-- Q03-P08 | claims: Q03-C10,Q03-C14 -->
The adopted Declaration of Geneva also attends to physicians' own health and abilities as conditions for care of a high standard.[4] I do not read that as an argument for exhausting the physician to protect the patient, or exhausting the patient to protect the physician.

<!-- Q03-P09 | claims: Q03-C12,Q03-C13 -->
I ask what the institution has organized so that neither must compensate indefinitely for work left unassigned. If a clinician lacks the means to fulfill a responsibility, identify the missing means and the person with authority to provide them. Do not let an institutional limitation return to the patient disguised as a personal failure to participate.

<!-- Q03-P10 | claims: Q03-C11,Q03-C14 -->
The World Health Organization's statement about the highest attainable standard of health continues with language against discrimination.[5] I do not turn that principle into a promise that every illness can be cured. I ask what is being done with the opportunities that remain.

<!-- Q03-P11 | claims: Q03-C13,Q03-C14 -->
A published commitment does not prove that it was fulfilled. Neither does a patient's suffering, by itself, establish which person failed which duty. That is why the examination must follow the work: what was needed, what was known, what was attempted, what prevented completion, and who remained responsible for the next step.

<!-- Q03-P12 | claims: Q03-C12,Q03-C13 -->
The question is not how many standards can be placed above my bed. It is which responsibilities have reached me—and which I am still being required to carry for the people and institutions entrusted with my care.
<!-- END CANDIDATE -->
