# HUEY-CL11 — economic burden, prevention and cost-shifting audit

**Huey book work.** Economics [#30](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/30); workload [#141](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/141); consequences/common good [#185](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/185); evidence phase [#326](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/326); institutional economics [#327](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/327); continuing development [PR #122](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/pull/122).

> Working draft. These are reproducible scenarios, not observed UCSF costs, a damages opinion, a forecast, a prevalence estimate, or proof that a particular accommodation would have prevented a particular event.

## Source-grounded starting point

The patient-authored September 17 response describes a burden-propagation sequence: unresolved access or communication can produce incomplete history, disputed records, correction work, continuity disputes, and repeated protective correspondence. It also states the narrower economic proposition that predictable uncertainty should be resolved at the lowest-cost point in the process. That is testimony and argument about this case; it is not a time sheet or institutional accounting record.

The source corpus separately asks whether the patient should have to perform the work of physician, lawyer, advocate, researcher, records custodian, accessibility engineer, coordinator, and investigator while still remaining the patient. The economic task is to separate the work that can actually be documented from illustrative replacement-cost scenarios and from lived loss that has no adequate market price.

## Correction to the earlier "$4,100 dispute" model

The earlier draft used a 28-hour internal-review scenario and 33 hours of patient/support-person time. It reported about **$3,301 institutional + $809 patient/family = $4,110**.

That number should no longer be used as a central estimate.

The 28 and 33 hours were explicitly hypothetical. The institutional calculation also mixed several different concepts: BLS wages, an assumed 35% employer load, and physician values closer to a direct-clinical opportunity-cost measure. Those are legitimate ingredients for sensitivity analysis, but they should not be presented as one homogeneous accounting cost.

Using the same hypothetical hours with clearly separated current benchmarks produces:

| Scenario | Institutional | Patient/family | Combined |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| BLS wage-only role mix | $1,911.93 | $808.82 | **$2,720.75** |
| Nonclinical labor scaled by the June 2026 BLS health-care wage/benefit ratio; 5 clinical physician hours at Doximity/AMA academic **all-work** value | $2,422.79 | $808.82 | **$3,231.61** |
| Same nonclinical sensitivity; 5 clinical physician hours valued as academic **direct-care capacity** | $3,151.91 | $808.82 | **$3,960.73** |
| Same nonclinical sensitivity; 5 clinical physician hours valued using the San Francisco/direct-care scenario | $3,410.73 | $808.82 | **$4,219.55** |

The old **$4,110** figure therefore falls inside a high-side opportunity-cost sensitivity range. It is not independently validated as the cost of a dispute, and it is not an observed institutional expenditure.

### What changed

- **BLS calls the occupational numbers wages, not compensation.** The earlier prose calling the lawyer figure "compensation" was imprecise.
- The May 2025 BLS median for all occupations is $50,980. Dividing by the OEWS standard 2,080-hour work year yields $24.51/hour. Using that for 33 patient/support-person hours yields $808.82. This remains an intentionally crude proxy; OEWS does not measure unpaid family work.
- The June 2026 BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation data report health-care/social-assistance wages of $35.13/hour and benefits of $14.80/hour. The ratio of total compensation to wages is about **1.4213**. Applying that broad industry ratio to the scenario is a sensitivity check, not a UCSF payroll estimate or a role-specific benefit calculation.
- Doximity reports **average physician compensation**, while the AMA reports average physician hours. Dividing one by the other creates an opportunity-cost proxy. It is not a payroll wage, a billable rate, or the marginal cost of one more minute.

## The approximately ten-dollar prevention model survives, with narrower language

The earlier model assumed:

1. a one-time three-minute physician note describing the relevant communication feature;
2. roughly 110 seconds of physician attention at a later encounter; and
3. one minute of staff preparation.

Using Doximity's 2025 academic-physician average compensation and the AMA's 2024 direct-patient-care hours gives an academic direct-care capacity value of about **$275.45/hour**. Using the June 2026 BLS employer-cost ratio with either the office/administrative-support or hospital medical-records wage benchmark produces a staff range of about **$32.42–$40.40/hour**.

On those assumptions:

- one-time three-minute physician note: **$13.77**;
- 110 seconds of physician direct-care capacity: **$8.42**;
- one minute of staff time: about **$0.54–$0.67**;
- recurring encounter scenario: about **$8.96–$9.09**.

Using the San Francisco Doximity physician-compensation figure yields about **$10.54–$10.67** per recurring encounter.

Thus "about ten dollars" remains a defensible order-of-magnitude scenario for this deliberately minimal layer. It does **not** establish that every effective accommodation costs ten dollars, that 110 seconds is clinically sufficient in every encounter, or that UCSF could have prevented any specific outcome for that amount.

At twelve modeled encounters per year over forty years, the academic scenario is about **$4,313–$4,377**, including the one-time note. That is a scenario, not a forecast of this patient's future care.

## Seven-year sensitivity: correct the center, keep the mechanism

The earlier seven-year example assumed six materially consequential failure cycles per year, or **42 cycles**. That event count was never asserted as historical fact.

With the same hypothetical cycle count:

| Per-cycle valuation | 42-cycle combined cost |
|---|---:|
| BLS wage-only role mix | **$114,271.50** |
| Academic all-work physician value + broad employer-load sensitivity | **$135,727.51** |
| Academic direct-care physician opportunity value + broad employer-load sensitivity | **$166,350.64** |
| San Francisco direct-care high-side sensitivity | **$177,221.08** |

Patient/support-person time remains **1,386 modeled hours** because that is simply 33 hours × 42 cycles.

The academic prevention scenario for 42 encounters is about **$390–$396**, including the one-time note. The corresponding ratio is therefore highly assumption-dependent—roughly hundreds to one, not one immutable **438:1** constant. The former $172,626 result is best understood as a high-side sensitivity calculation rather than a central estimate.

## Forty-year sensitivity: retain only as a stress test

At one modeled dispute cycle per month for forty years, the scenario contains **480 cycles** and **15,840 patient/support-person hours**.

Depending on the valuation basis above, the combined labor scenario ranges from about **$1.31 million** to **$2.03 million**. The academic prevention scenario at twelve encounters per year for forty years is about **$4.31–$4.38 thousand**; the San Francisco scenario is about **$5.07–$5.14 thousand**.

These figures demonstrate linear sensitivity to repeated cycles. They do not establish that 480 events will occur, that forty years of care will be delivered, that every event would trigger the modeled review, or that any modeled dollar amount is legally recoverable.

The earlier "fully compliant adversarial equilibrium" language should therefore remain reference analysis rather than a factual prediction.

## The national hospital model is not an outpatient-neurology ROI

Hurtig, Alper, and Berkowitz published a 2018 hospital model using national hospital admissions, adverse-event rates/costs, communication-barrier prevalence assumptions, and the Bartlett risk estimate. Their middle estimate was **671,440 fewer preventable adverse-event cases and $6.8 billion in annual cost reduction** if communication barriers were reduced as modeled.

That result is a model of hospitalized populations and four selected adverse events. The paper itself used older underlying national inputs, including AHRQ 2013 adverse-event data and AHA 2016 hospital-admission data.

It should support only a bounded proposition: communication barriers can have large modeled downstream costs in hospital care. It does not supply:
- the ROI of a UCSF outpatient neurology accommodation;
- the probability of harm in this patient's care;
- the value of the author's requested pathway;
- a local savings estimate; or
- a national 2026 outpatient-neurology forecast.

## The 15-million-visit neurology scale is historical

The CDC/NCHS fact sheet reports an estimated **15 million annual visits** to nonfederally employed, office-based neurologists in **2015–2016**. That is a historical utilization denominator, not a current 2026 visit count.

The earlier 5%, 10%, and 20% communication-modification scenarios were assumptions. They should not be described as prevalence estimates. The later 0.1% and 1% dispute-rate examples were likewise sensitivity calculations.

Those national scaling examples may remain in reference material if every assumed rate is visibly labeled. They should not carry the main economic argument.

## What can be said with confidence

The strongest economic proposition does not require a multimillion-dollar model:

**A cost is not eliminated merely because it is transferred outside the institution's ledger.**

For each claimed burden transfer, record separately:

- the task;
- who ordinarily or actually performed it;
- whether the task was necessary because of illness, ordinary participation, or a correctable process failure;
- patient time;
- support-person time;
- institutional/staff time;
- duplication;
- delay;
- direct out-of-pocket expense where actually documented;
- the evidence linking one step to the next;
- feasible interruption points; and
- effects that are reported but not monetized.

The patient's September response describes upstream uncertainty becoming downstream writing. That is a source-supported mechanism claim to investigate. It is not proof that every later task was avoidable.

NICE's multimorbidity guideline supplies a separate clinical concept of **treatment burden**, including appointments, treatments, effects on daily life, coordination, and patient priorities. It is UK guidance, not the legal standard governing UCSF. Its relevance is narrower: care can appropriately assess the work that care itself requires.

## What remains deliberately unpriced

Do not turn the following into a wage calculation merely because the book discusses economics:

- pain;
- fear;
- loss of trust;
- diminished family participation;
- worship;
- civic life;
- concentration;
- recovery time;
- the patient's limited usable attention;
- the support person's relationship to the patient;
- dignity; or
- the value of a life.

Replacement-cost or opportunity-cost scenarios can make transferred labor visible. They do not measure those losses.
