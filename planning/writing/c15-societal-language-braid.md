## B1 — Naming: the suffix is not the evidence

I have spent this book asking what happens when a word arrives before the person.

A word can enter a record first. It can enter a room first. It can cross an institution faster than the correction that follows it. By the time the human being arrives, the word may already be waiting.

Some of the heaviest words I know travel with small endings.

*Racism. Racist.*

*Socialism. Socialist.*

*Communism. Communist.*

*Fascism. Fascist.*

*Capitalism. Capitalist.*

*Ableism. Ableist.*

The endings are almost nothing. Three letters. Four. A small turn of grammar by which an action can become a system, a system can become a person, and a person can become what the word seems to have decided.

Sometimes the word is necessary.

Sometimes history would be falsified by refusing it.

Sometimes politeness is simply a way of asking the injured person to carry one more burden so that everyone else may remain comfortable.

But the opposite error is available too. A grave word can arrive so quickly that the relation it is supposed to describe never catches up.

That is the danger I have tried to describe in the medical record. The inherited sentence is already loaded. The patient's correction still has to travel.

Language can behave the same way.

The label is cached.

The person is not.

I do not want to solve that problem by making the words illegal. I want the opposite. I want words strong enough to name what is real. I want *racism* if the relations support racism. I want *assault* if the facts support assault. I want *communist*, *socialist*, *fascist*, *capitalist*, *ableist*, *racist* and every other serious word to remain available to the future.

But I want their provenance attached.

What definition?

What act?

What relation?

What evidence?

Whose description?

What remains disputed?

What changed when the word was applied?

The suffix cannot do that work for us.

Similarity of sound cannot do it either. *Community*, *communion*, *communal*, *communism* and *socialism* may live near one another in language while carrying very different histories, institutions and economic claims. A political movement does not become equivalent to another merely because a familiar syllable survives inside its name.

The word is where inquiry may begin.

It is not where inquiry has to end.

## B2 — Power: the office speaks too

As I write, the President of the United States provides a public example of the reciprocity I am trying to understand.

In April 2026, President Donald Trump said that Democratic “hate speech” was dangerous for the country. [LP01] That proposition deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms: political language can have consequences, and an officeholder does not lose the right to object to language directed against him merely because he is powerful.

The principle becomes interesting when it turns around.

In June 2026, Trump publicly called Democrats “Dumocrats” and said they were dumb. [LP02] During the 2026 campaign season he used *Communist* and *Socialist* against particular Democratic and progressive candidates; Reuters also reported that Republican campaigns themselves were divided over how useful that ideological attack was. [LP03] In November 2025 he called six Democratic lawmakers “traitors,” described their conduct as seditious and wrote that it could be punishable by death; the White House later said he was not seeking their execution. [LP04]

I do not need to decide a political verdict about him here.

I need only preserve the rule.

If hostile rhetoric used against a President can be consequential, language used by a President can be consequential too.

That is reciprocity.

The same problem appears in the word *fight*.

On January 6, 2021, Trump told supporters that they had to “fight like hell.” In the same speech he also told them to march “peacefully and patriotically” and make their voices heard. [LP05]

Both belong to the record.

Effect does not retroactively become dictionary definition.

Intent does not erase effect.

Context does not mean selecting whichever surrounding words protect the conclusion we already wanted.

The responsible question is larger: what was said, by whom, to whom, in what circumstances, with what reasonably available meanings, and what can actually be established about what followed?

I have asked medicine to preserve exactly those distinctions.

A patient's sentence is not a diagnosis merely because it is severe.

A physician's sentence does not become true merely because it entered the chart.

An institutional notice does not become understood merely because it was sent.

And a metaphor does not become a clinical finding because somebody repeats it later.

Office changes none of those rules.

Office changes the scale at which mistakes can travel.

A patient's word can be ignored.

A physician's word can enter a medical record.

A clinic's word can condition access.

A regulator's word can alter a license.

A court's word can become judgment.

A President's word can cross the country before the sentence has finished leaving his mouth.

That is why I think the burden of language should rise with entrusted power.

Not because powerful people deserve fewer words.

Because authority is an amplifier.

The right to speak survives amplification.

So should responsibility for what the amplified word carries.

## B3 — Societal confession: custody returns to me

And then the rule returns to the author.

I have used enormous words in these pages.

*Abuse.*

*Assault.*

*Battery.*

*Abandonment.*

*Trauma.*

*Trust.*

*Crime.*

*Racism.*

*Violence.*

*Love.*

*Mercy.*

*Justice.*

I cannot ask a physician to preserve the difference between my statement and his interpretation while pretending my own interpretation has no source.

I cannot ask an institution to preserve context and then remove context when a sentence belongs to someone I oppose.

I cannot ask a regulator to distinguish allegation from finding while using accusation as though saying it had completed the investigation.

I cannot object when another person turns my metaphor into pathology and then turn my metaphor about somebody else into a diagnosis.

The discipline has to survive contact with me.

That is the confession at the societal scale.

I do not want a future frightened of serious language.

I want a survivor to be able to say *rape*.

I want a witness to be able to say *racism*.

I want a citizen to be able to say *fascism*, *socialism*, *communism*, *capitalism*, *ableism*, *sexism* or any other classification when the word is the one the evidence can responsibly bear.

I want satire.

I want anger.

I want the four-letter word when four letters are enough.

What I do not want is a grammar in which my words receive context because they are mine while yours are stripped of context because I have power over the record in which they will survive.

That is what this book has been asking of doctors.

It is what it has been asking of institutions.

It is what it has been asking of police, regulators, lawyers, universities and the State.

It has to be what it asks of a President.

It has to be what it asks of a newspaper.

It has to be what it asks of a political movement.

And it has to be what it asks of me.

A supported grave word should not be weakened merely because it embarrasses power.

An unsupported grave word should not be strengthened merely because it humiliates an opponent.

Evidence may sustain the word.

Evidence may narrow it.

Evidence may contradict it.

Some words may remain only as faithful testimony about what an event felt like to the person who lived it.

That is not censorship.

It is custody.

Confession does not absolve the speaker from that custody.

If anything, confession begins it.

So when the words ending in *-ism* and *-ist* finally arrive at the reader, I do not want them to arrive alone.

I want the people attached.

I want the acts attached.

I want the record attached.

I want the uncertainty attached.

I want the power attached.

I want the possibility of correction attached.

I want the right to use the word preserved by the obligation to carry what makes the word true.

That is the social responsibility of confession.

The right to speak and the duty to answer for speech should rise together.

And where speech is joined to entrusted authority, the duty should become heavier, not lighter.

I have asked other people to bear that weight.

Now I have to leave my own words where you can examine them.
