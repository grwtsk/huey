# Confession, antiracist practice and an open reader question

Companion to [LG-01](README.md). Work [#105](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/105); narrative integration [#51](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/51). This is a comparison of public texts, not the author's private confession or an assessment of the book's participants.

## Four distinct contributions

### Zadie Smith: the reader remains another person

In the Foreword to *Feel Free*, signed January 18, 2017 and published in the 2018 collection, Smith asks: **“I feel this—do you?”** She leaves the reader free to reject the experience she offers and situates writing among language, world and self. This supplies a dialogical relationship, not another person's answer. [CD01](confession-dialogue-sources.json)

Editorial use: a direct question can carry force without supplying the reader's reply. Do not import Smith's characterization of her own intimate evidence as the genre or evidentiary status of this book. Do not imitate her sentences or claim her endorsement. No earlier particular Smith text was established from the project search; the Foreword is the specific published framing selected for this comparison.

### Ibram X. Kendi: acknowledgment oriented toward change

In Rob Brunner's 2019 interview, Kendi calls **“the heartbeat of antiracism”** confession and describes racist/antiracist terms as applying to what a person says or does in a moment rather than a fixed total identity. His Berkeley talk connects ideas, power, policy, inequity and action or inaction. The first source emphasizes self-examination; the second keeps structural change in view and explicitly proposes a sharper racist/antiracist opposition. That last proposal remains his, not a forced reader verdict in this book. [CD02–CD03](confession-dialogue-sources.json)

Editorial use: preserve an explicitly supplied self-description without revising it into permanent essence, compelled purity or an automatic diagnosis of someone else. The personal source is not evidence that the author accepts every part of Kendi's framework.

### Ijeoma Oluo: opposition does not establish freedom from racism

In her 2018 interview with Chinelo Nkechi Ikem, Oluo distinguishes being antiracist from being racism-free. She treats guilt as an emotion, not an accomplishment that itself helps others. She also stresses that Black women's knowledge and work must receive recognition and support rather than being limited to a few permitted voices. [CD04](confession-dialogue-sources.json)

Editorial use: do not make another person's love, labor or participation a certificate of the narrator's innocence. The reader's discomfort is not the final measure of what the passage accomplishes.

### Sara Ahmed: admitting something is not automatically undoing it

Ahmed's 2004 article examines declarations that turn acknowledgment of bad practice into a sign of good practice. The Goldsmiths abstract states the central problem; the inspected French translation, especially numbered paragraphs 12 and 17–23, develops the distinction between recognition, institutional action and self-transcendence. [CD05–CD06](confession-dialogue-sources.json)

Editorial use: the admission must not perform a narrative shortcut from acknowledgment to acquittal. This is a question about what declarations do, not evidence that a particular speaker is insincere.

## Preserve the tension, rather than manufacturing consensus

The following is editorial synthesis. These writers have not jointly endorsed it: confession can initiate attention without completing change; a reader can be addressed without being conscripted; and the act of naming remains answerable to the relations it names. Kendi's affirmative use of confession and Ahmed's criticism of what admissions can become are kept in conversation, not presented as the same argument.

The author has now supplied his own self-attribution. RF-01 prohibits the assistant from inventing it, not the author from making it. Keeping this new statement does not retroactively turn previous narration into a categorical accusation or make one person's inner experience evidence of another's thoughts. Neither does the research require a euphemistic replacement of the actual self-description. The source, the theory and the proposed adaptation remain three distinct records.


## Societal responsibility: classification, authority and confession

The author's later instruction under [#332](https://github.com/grwtsk/huey/issues/332) widens the capstone without moving it. The confession now has to ask what happens when serious classification words move through society and through institutions with unequal power.

The editorial rule is reciprocal rather than partisan. A consequential label can be necessary, supported and morally important; it can also become a shortcut that outruns its definition and evidence. The `-ism` / `-ist` morphology does not decide which is which. Preserve the underlying act, relation, source, scope, dispute and human referent.

The public [language/power source record](language-power-sources.json) supplies current political examples because presidential speech makes amplification unusually visible. It preserves both the President's claim that hostile rhetoric used against him can be dangerous and examples of his own use of ridicule and ideological labels. The January 6 transcript is retained with both the “fight like hell” and “peacefully and patriotically” language. These examples test reciprocity; they do not establish motive, fitness, election outcome, unlawful incitement or any other legal conclusion.

The literary movement must return to the narrator. Huey's own grave words remain subject to the same custody: supported words should not be euphemized for comfort; unsupported words should not be strengthened for effect. The societal extension therefore deepens rather than replaces the original capstone's anti-absolution rule. Confession creates responsibility for what a declaration does next.

The final reader question remains private and unchanged. Do not insert political citations, a prescribed answer or an adult explanatory sentence after it.
