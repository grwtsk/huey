### The pivot: response, capacity, and care

Everything above reduces to two linked questions.

**Did my disclosure of suicide risk lead to an identifiable clinical response?**

**Did the system equip the treating physician to provide, obtain, coordinate, or escalate that response?**

Those questions must be answered separately before they are joined. A patient can be left without an effective response because a physician did not use an available pathway, because the pathway did not equip the physician to act, because responsibility failed at a handoff, because several failures interacted, or because a response occurred that has not yet been identified to me. The record should determine which explanation the evidence supports.

**I am not asking medicine to become something other than medicine. I am asking the institutions through which it operates to maintain the connections necessary to protect life. An administrative boundary may divide responsibility. It must not be allowed to make responsibility disappear.**

That is a statement of humility and scope. A neurologist need not become a psychiatrist. A regulator need not become a treating physician. One clinic need not assume authority it does not possess. But when a material risk to life reaches one boundary, the boundary should make the next responsibility visible and preserve the connection until an appropriate recipient has actually received it.

This is where the letter turns. Preservation remains necessary, but preservation is no longer its final purpose.

### From defense to construction: what medicine must be able to connect

There is a point at which a record built defensively must become constructive.

### Do not repair medicine by destroying its physicians

If clinicians or staff carry conduct they cannot reconcile with their professional obligations, create a path by which they may step away.

Provide confidential evaluation, mental-health treatment, moral-injury support, disability support where appropriate, professional remediation, time, income protection where law and employment structures permit it, and an independently evaluated route back to practice where return can occur safely.

A leave should not be a confession of wrongdoing. Treatment should not be punishment. Remediation should not erase accountability. And accountability should not require us to pretend that physicians cease being human when they obtain a medical license.

Where independent investigation establishes conduct incompatible with continued practice, the competent authority must act. Where a clinician can be rehabilitated safely, medicine should prefer restoration to ceremonial destruction.

The pandemic gives this request additional urgency. It did not injure every clinician or patient in the same way, but CDC data document substantial deterioration in health-worker well-being, including increased burnout, fatigue, loss, and grief, while the Surgeon General has called health-worker burnout a systemic problem affecting the ability of patients to obtain care. [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/vitalsigns/health-worker-mental-health/index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

So examine the physician as carefully as you examine the patient.

Ask what they were carrying.

Ask what their institution required.

Ask what it prevented.

Ask what they knew.

Ask what they could do.

Ask what they failed to do.

Ask what they need before they should again exercise authority over another human being.

And when physicians have passed through extraordinary strain without surrendering their humanity, recognize that too. I know physicians whom I would nominate for such recognition by name. ### Never again should an institutional boundary make protection disappear

The rule I propose is simple:

**No institution bearing responsibility for medical care may treat the boundary between organizations, departments, clinics, records, or individual professionals as the point at which responsibility for protecting life disappears.**

The Medical Board may regulate.

UCSF may treat.

Psychiatry may have one scope.

Neurology may have another.

A hospital may operate one record system.

A physician may reasonably lack authority over another service.

Those differences matter.

But a person does not become several different people because the organizational chart has several boxes.

When a grave safety concern arrives at the wrong box, the proper response is not disappearance. It is an accountable handoff.

That means, at minimum: receipt; identification of the proper destination; transmission of sufficient information; acknowledgment by a human recipient; assignment of the next responsibility; an escalation path if the handoff fails; and communication back to the patient through a channel the patient can actually use.

That is the connective tissue I ask these institutions to build.

It is comparatively inexpensive when measured against the human stakes. I do not pretend to know its exact cost or claim a quantified risk reduction that has not been measured. I ask UCSF and the other institutions with the relevant data to calculate both.

What does it cost to close the loop?

What does it cost not to?
