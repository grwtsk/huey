# Standard
*R.A. Jacob Martone*

**Is it standard for the patient to be the doctor, the lawyer, the advocate, the researcher, the records custodian, the accessibility engineer, the care coordinator, and the investigator—and still have enough strength left to be the patient?**

I am not claiming those professional titles. I am asking why so much of the work associated with them has become necessary for me to obtain, understand, and preserve my care.

What is an institution that claims to care doing when the person seeking relief must also construct the clinical chronology, identify the unanswered questions, reconcile the specialties, explain the communication disability, research the applicable obligations, correct the resulting record, and design the process by which someone might finally become responsible?

Does anyone inside the institution ask that question?

Not whether the patient has written too much. Not whether the patient has become difficult to answer. Not whether another boundary should be imposed.

Whether the institution has made the patient responsible for work that its own claim to provide care should have required it to organize.

And if the answer is that this arrangement is *standard*, then the word itself needs examination.

## What kind of standard?

A standard can be a customary practice: what usually happens. It can be a criterion: the basis against which something is judged. It can identify an acceptable level of performance, an authoritative reference, a rule of measurement, or a recognized model of excellence. These meanings coexist in the dictionary. They do not become interchangeable merely because an institution uses the same word for all of them. [Source-review lead S1: dictionary senses of standard; SOC-C010–C017, #128.]

Custom describes conduct. A criterion evaluates it.

An institution may have a standard appointment length without having established that the length permits an adequate appointment in every circumstance. It may have a standard communication channel without having established that the channel works for the person required to use it. It may have a standard form without having established that the form can receive the information necessary to answer its own questions.

“This is what we ordinarily do” is the beginning of an explanation. It is not the conclusion of an evaluation.

The professional standard of care is more specific. California’s civil jury instructions describe it in terms of the skill, knowledge, and care that reasonably careful practitioners would use in comparable circumstances; the corresponding specialist instruction applies that comparison within the relevant specialty. The reference is not merely to what this clinic prefers. The circumstances matter. [Source-review lead S2: applicable California professional/specialist jury instructions and versions; SOC-C013–C014, #128.]

That does not make every desired treatment mandatory. It does not turn every disappointing outcome into negligence. It does, however, make an individualized question unavoidable: what did reasonable care require **here**, given the information available and the circumstances of this patient?

An internal procedure is a different thing again. It organizes how work is performed. A legal obligation identifies an enforceable requirement under applicable law. An ethical standard states how professional responsibility should be exercised. An institutional commitment tells patients what the organization represents itself as providing.

One may inform another. None should silently replace another.

Nor does calling something a *gold standard* establish that it is legally required. That phrase can identify a benchmark against which alternatives are compared; it cannot manufacture evidence for a treatment, prove suitability for an individual, or transform a proposed arrangement of services into a universally binding protocol. [Source-review lead S3: gold-standard lexical meaning and its limits; SOC-C017, #128.]

I therefore ask the institution to identify the standard it invokes. Is this a legal requirement, a professional judgment, an evidence-based recommendation, an internal procedure, a resource limitation, or simply the customary way the office operates?

Then identify what follows from it.

The authority of the word should not exceed the authority of its source.

## Standard procedure is not a standard patient

The September 9 clinic notice repeatedly invokes standard communication channels and clinical processes. It also offers additional time within visits, explanation before examination, an after-visit summary, and telemedicine when appropriate. Those offered measures belong in the account; I do not erase them because other questions remain. But the same notice says that clinicians will continue communicating verbally and places limits on written preparation and specially structured processes. The issue is whether the offered measures answer the actual communication need—not merely whether accommodations appear somewhere in the letter. [Source-review lead S4: complete September 9 notice, pages 1–2; SOC-C103–C104, #138.]

I have described that need. The difficulty is not exhausted by getting already-formed words out of my mouth. It can involve receiving a question, understanding its scope, consolidating an answer, recognizing what needs qualification, and expressing the result. I requested consequential questions in writing when necessary, one proposition at a time, with processing time and an opportunity to complete or qualify an answer. [Source-review lead S5: September 17 response, communication section; SOC-C101/C107, #138.]

An institution cannot answer that description simply by pointing to the standard way it talks.

The Department of Justice’s effective-communication guidance addresses this distinction directly. The relevant considerations include the nature, length, complexity, and context of the communication and the person’s usual communication methods. Its purpose is effective exchange, not the ceremonial availability of a channel. The guidance also places responsibility for effective communication on covered entities rather than allowing them simply to require a patient to bring an interpreter. [Source-review lead S6: DOJ effective-communication guidance and applicable limits; SOC-C105–C106/C111, #138.]

Uniformity is sameness of method. It is not necessarily equality of participation.

A modified method may be precisely what allows the underlying standard to remain intact. More time may preserve accuracy. Written questions may preserve comprehension. A pause may preserve consent. An alternative delivery route may preserve continuity.

The standard should protect the purpose of the encounter, not merely the familiar shape of the encounter.

Consent supplies another example. The AMA describes informed consent as a communication process resulting in authorization of a particular intervention, with attention to understanding, voluntary decision-making, relevant information, and documentation. A signature may document that process. It is not a substitute for the process. [Source-review lead S7: AMA informed-consent opinion; SOC-C123–C124, #140.]

The question is therefore not simply whether I remained in the room, followed an instruction, or failed to interrupt rapidly. It is what communication occurred, what I understood, what I authorized, and whether I had an effective means of expressing uncertainty or stopping.

There is also the standard of language. *Standard English* describes established conventions of speech and writing. It does not establish a standard of human credibility. A grammatically impeccable sentence can be false. A misspelled sentence can report an event accurately. [Source-review lead S8: lexical convention versus evaluative inference; SOC-C019, #128.]

I care about precision. I have spent substantial effort qualifying what I mean. But precision should help receive the patient’s information, not become an entrance examination that the patient must pass before the information is considered.

When I say I could most easily circle the entire pain form, ask what I mean. When I say that I can stand but that standing brings great pain, preserve both parts of the sentence.

What is being standardized: the reliability of the clinical information, or the outward performance of an acceptable patient?

## The professions that arrive in the bed

I am largely bedbound. I am not paralyzed. The ability to stand briefly does not describe the cost of standing, just as the ability to produce a document does not establish an unlimited capacity to produce documents.

Yet the work continues to accumulate.

I provide the history. That is participation. But must I also discover which unanswered clinical questions have fallen between specialties, identify the possible interactions, determine what further evaluation might be relevant, and persuade someone to assume responsibility for evaluating it?

That is the point at which participation begins to resemble substitution.

I am not asking to prescribe my own treatment. I am asking why the work of making a clinical question actionable so often returns to the person who brought the question because he needed clinical help.

Then comes the legal work. I must distinguish acknowledgment of receipt from agreement with disputed language. I must explain that refusal of an inaccessible format is not refusal of medicine. I must preserve the distinction between an allegation and an adjudicated conclusion, between an amendment request and a demand that someone accept my account as proven. Those distinctions are expressly present in my correspondence. [Source-review lead S9: September 17 response, receipt/compliance sections; #127/#140.]

I have not asked the neurologist to become my lawyer. My September response expressly separates investigation and adjudication of alleged wrongdoing from the continuing neurological assessment of clinically relevant history. [Source-review lead S10: September 17 response, prior encounters section; SOC-C131, #140.]

But a proper boundary around the physician’s role should not become a boundary around the patient’s history.

A fact does not cease to be medically relevant because it may also be legally consequential.

Then comes advocacy: identifying the appropriate office, explaining why the question belongs there, requesting routing, seeking confirmation, and determining whether a review actually exists. My September response went so far as to include a recipient-routing form asking the recipient to identify their role, any onward referral, and the responsible contact. The patient had prepared a mechanism for the institution to account for its own handling of his request. [Source-review lead S11: September 17 response, recipient compliance/routing confirmation; SOC-C134/C144, #141.]

Then comes records work: preserving dates, separating sources, identifying omissions, comparing statements, and requesting correction without allowing the correction to erase the original disagreement.

Then comes accessibility engineering: describing the communication problem, proposing a usable sequence, supplying technology, and explaining why the technology cannot help if the encounter proceeds too quickly to permit its use.

Then comes coordination: following a recommendation beyond the entry of the order to determine whether it became a service I could actually receive.

The AMA’s referral guidance assigns physicians responsibilities concerning the medical basis of a referral, appropriate professional expertise, information sharing, and explanation of findings and recommendations. Referral is a professional act with content and responsibilities, not merely a destination handed to the patient. [Source-review lead S12: AMA consultation/referral opinion; SOC-C118, #139.]

Each task may be defended separately as a small request. Together they form a second occupation.

And my wife is drawn into it. In my August letter, I described her work as driver, attendant, advocate, communication support, records custodian, witness, and protector. I also described the professional work displaced by surviving, documenting disability, reconstructing events, and pursuing an accountable response. [Source-review lead S13: August 4 letter, life affected; SOC-C134/C142, #141.]

**My wife’s love is not an institutional staffing plan. My ability to write is not an unlimited administrative resource.**

Patient agency means that I retain a meaningful role in decisions about my life. It does not mean that every unassigned responsibility in the system becomes mine.

## The standards I have already brought

I have not asked that my assertions be exempt from scrutiny. I have asked that scrutiny be disciplined.

A scientific standard requires that an observation remain distinguishable from an explanation. A temporal association should not be promoted into causation merely because it offers a compelling story. A genetic finding should retain its actual classification. A proposed mechanism should remain proposed until the evidence supports more.

The same discipline applies to institutional explanations. A diagnostic label does not, by itself, establish why a particular change occurred at a particular time. “Standard process” does not establish that the process worked. “Appropriate care” is a conclusion that should be connected to an assessment, not a phrase that makes the assessment unnecessary.

A measurement standard provides a reference against which a result can be established. NIST’s account of metrological traceability requires a documented chain connecting a measurement result to a specified reference, with uncertainty accounted for. It also warns that traceability alone does not guarantee fitness for a particular purpose. [Source-review lead S14: NIST metrological traceability; SOC-C020, #128.]

I invoke that as an analogy, not as a claim that every clinical conversation must become laboratory calibration. The relevant discipline is that a result needs a basis, its limitations matter, and an authoritative label does not do the work of validation.

When I ask for *traceable clinical diligence*, I ask for the clinical counterpart: what question was considered, what information was available, what was observed, what could not be evaluated, what conclusion was reached, and what remained to be done. Those are the stages identified in my September response. [Source-review lead S15: September 17 response, traceable clinical diligence; #127/#139.]

An evidentiary standard also requires provenance. My statement is my statement. My wife’s observation is hers. A clinician’s observation is different from an assertion inherited from a previous note. Several repetitions of one assertion do not become several independent observations. My September 10 letter expressly preserves that distinction. [Source-review lead S16: September 10 letter, section 3; SOC-C125–C126, #140.]

A standard of proof asks a different question: what degree of evidentiary persuasion is required for a particular decision? California’s civil instructions distinguish the ordinary more-likely-than-not burden from clear-and-convincing proof, which requires high probability; they separately recognize the criminal requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. These are not interchangeable thresholds. [Source-review lead S17: applicable California proof instructions and versions; SOC-C021, #128.]

Neither should the threshold for a conviction become the entrance requirement for a clinical inquiry.

I can require that allegations remain unadjudicated while still asking that relevant symptoms be assessed. I can preserve uncertainty about cause while requesting relief and protection from further harm. I can ask for investigation without asking the investigator to guarantee my conclusion.

I have also applied a standard of proportionality to my own demands. I have acknowledged reasonable limits on submission length and timing. I have distinguished my requested thirty-day resolution period from a statutory deadline. I have asked each recipient to act within their actual authority rather than assume powers they do not possess. [Source-review lead S18: September 17 response, preparation and requested-action sections; #127/#140.]

Those qualifications are not incidental. They show that I am trying to make the demand answerable.

But no patient should have to demonstrate this degree of legal, scientific, and administrative discipline merely to deserve an adequate response. A less articulate patient, a less educated patient, an exhausted patient, or a patient unable to complete the next letter should not thereby become less eligible for care.

The question is not whether I have become an unusually capable advocate.

It is what happens to the person who cannot.

## Professional standards cannot travel in only one direction

UCSF’s published PRIDE values describe professionalism through competence, accountability, reliability, responsibility, and collaboration. They connect integrity with truthfulness, respect with consideration of others, diversity with inclusion, and excellence with sustained effort and support for others. Those are institutional representations, not definitions I invented for this dispute. [Source-review lead S19: UCSF mission/values and relevant versions; SOC-C145, #142.]

I ask what they look like in action.

Competence should be visible in an assessment. Accountability should identify a responsible person. Reliability should appear in completed commitments. Collaboration should be evident where responsibilities meet. Integrity should preserve the difference between what is known and what is merely asserted.

Respect includes how people speak to one another. It cannot be exhausted by tone.

A courteous response may still leave the clinical question unanswered. A severe criticism may still identify an actual failure. The severity of a sentence and the adequacy of the conduct it describes require separate examination.

This does not remove my obligations. The AMA’s account of patient responsibilities includes truthfulness, provision of history as fully as possible, collaboration with agreed treatment, disclosure when a plan needs reconsideration, and avoidance of disruptive conduct. It also includes reporting illegal or unethical professional behavior to appropriate bodies. Participation is not defined solely as agreement. [Source-review lead S20: AMA patient-responsibilities opinion; SOC-C146, #142.]

I accept scrutiny of my conduct. I ask that the relevant conduct, context, and evidence be identified.

A *double standard* is an unequal application of a rule or principle to different people or groups. Different professional roles are not themselves a double standard. The problem arises when a shared requirement—accuracy, specificity, accountability, or respect—is exacted from the patient but treated as unnecessary when the institution speaks. [Source-review lead S21: lexical definition and case-specific comparator; SOC-C022/C150, #128/#142.]

If I must distinguish inference from fact, the institution should do so.

If I must specify what I am alleging, the institution should specify what conduct it attributes to me.

If I must explain why a proposed route cannot be used, the institution should explain how its proposed alternative answers the obstacle.

If my continuing participation depends on defined conditions, the conditions should be intelligible enough to follow and the process for interpreting them identifiable enough to review.

The September 9 notice makes agreement within sixty days a condition of remaining in the clinic and permits that agreement through a signature or MyChart message. That makes the substance and interpretation of the conditions consequential regardless of the mechanism used to communicate agreement. [Source-review lead S22: September 9 notice, page 3; SOC-C148, #142.]

Meanwhile, UCSF’s public patient-rights statement recognizes the right to raise concerns without compromising care or future access and to receive a reasonable, timely response. It also identifies a right to know who has primary responsibility for coordinating care. These commitments do not eliminate legitimate safety requirements. They make it necessary to distinguish the exercise of a right from conduct that independently justifies restriction. [Source-review lead S23: UCSF patient rights and relevant version; SOC-C149/C152, #142.]

A standard that becomes exacting when directed at the patient and indistinct when directed at the institution is not yet a reciprocal standard.

It is an asymmetry requiring an explanation.

## What is the institution doing with the burden?

When necessary work is left undone until the patient performs it, the work has not disappeared. Its cost has moved.

When the patient must detect the missing handoff, reconstruct the history, correct the misunderstanding, identify the responsible office, and verify that action occurred, the institution has transferred work and risk to the person seeking care.

That describes the function of the arrangement. It does not require pretending to know anyone’s motive.

My September response described the sequence: an unresolved accommodation produces an incomplete history; the incomplete history becomes a disputed note; the disputed note requires correction; a failed referral produces a continuity dispute; an unusable communication route generates more protective correspondence. The burden becomes visible in the patient’s writing, even when the event that generated it occurred elsewhere. [Source-review lead S24: September 17 response, upstream burden section; SOC-C136–C137, #141.]

That distinction is fundamental.

The patient may be carrying the complexity without having created it.

An institution cannot adequately assess the burden of reading my correspondence while refusing to assess the burden of making that correspondence necessary.

Some work accompanies illness. Some work is necessary to receive treatment. Some administrative work is unavoidable. But corrective work generated by preventable failures is a separate category. It should not be folded into “patient complexity” and treated as though it were another feature of the disease.

There is established clinical language for treatment burden. NICE’s multimorbidity guideline directs attention to how treatments affect daily life, including the number and location of appointments, medication demands, adverse effects, and effects on well-being. That is United Kingdom clinical guidance, not a declaration of the legal standard governing UCSF. Its relevance is that the work imposed by care is itself a legitimate object of clinical assessment. [Source-review lead S25: NICE NG56, recommendations and version; SOC-C143, #141.]

The supplied medical-trauma overview likewise emphasizes predictability, autonomy, coordination, and pacing. As an informational document, it does not establish every intervention required in my case. But its organizing premise is incompatible with ignoring the conditions under which treatment must be received. [Source-review lead S26: supplied two-page informational overview, not a professional guideline; #127/#131.]

A plan should therefore account not only for what it asks me to do, but for what doing it consumes.

There is a standard of efficiency at issue. An office may reduce its own workload by returning an unresolved task to the patient. That does not establish that the total work has decreased. It may merely place the work where it is less visible and less likely to appear in the institution’s accounting.

There is a standard of value at issue. Time spent reconstructing a disputed encounter is time unavailable for something else. In my case, I have described the effect on family life, professional work, worship, and ordinary participation. Those losses should not disappear simply because the patient’s labor carries no invoice. [Source-review lead S27: August 4 letter, life affected; SOC-C138/C142, #141.]

And there is a standard of living at issue: not merely whether the body remains alive, but what life remains available within its limitations.

If my usable attention is consumed proving that I need a usable encounter, what remains for the encounter?

If my limited energy is spent coordinating the people meant to help me, what remains for treatment, recovery, or family?

If the institution’s process works only because I repeatedly repair it, what happens when I cannot complete the repair?

The appropriate measure is not simply whether I managed to submit another letter.

It is whether another letter should have been necessary.

## Does anyone ask?

I do not know what any particular person has considered privately. I am asking for the consideration that became action—or for an account of why it did not.

Did anyone assess the combined demands being placed on me rather than evaluating each demand in isolation?

Did anyone distinguish the complexity of my illness from the complexity generated by the way my care was organized?

Did anyone ask whether a repeated request reflected a new demand or an old question that had not received an answer?

Did anyone ask what the patient was doing that a clinician, records service, accessibility function, coordinator, or independent reviewer should have been doing?

Most importantly: **did anyone accept responsibility for reducing that burden?**

A sincere private concern may matter morally. It does not, by itself, complete a referral, correct a record, establish an accommodation, or make a treatment plan usable.

I am not asking the institution to demonstrate an emotion. I am asking it to demonstrate the work.

That examination must remain capable of finding problems above and around the individual clinician. If a physician lacked time, authority, information, or an effective route for obtaining help, that belongs in the account. My September response expressly asks that investigation follow the decisions rather than decide in advance whether responsibility ends with the physician, clinic, cross-clinic process, or wider institution. [Source-review lead S28: September 17 response, institutional constraint and investigation; SOC-C120/C161, #139/#143.]

An institution should not leave the patient to compensate for an unsupported physician, then leave that physician alone to answer for the institution.

The standard of review must be adequate to the level of the question.

Who made the decision? What information was available? What alternatives were considered? What was communicated? Who received responsibility for the next step? What evidence shows that the step occurred?

These are answerable questions. They do not require disclosure of every confidential deliberation. They require enough information to distinguish an actual review from reassurance that someone, somewhere, has reviewed something.

My requested remedy is correspondingly concrete: an accessible encounter, an identifiable plan, accurate attribution, assigned responsibility, a workable route for unresolved matters, and review that can be verified. My September response expressly states that the objective is to reduce adversarial scrutiny as the reliability of the underlying system becomes demonstrable. [Source-review lead S29: September 17 response, continuing review and requested-action sections; #138/#140/#142.]

I do not want the work of institutional oversight to become my permanent occupation.

I want the institution to become reliable enough that I can stop doing it.

## A standard must be able to bear weight

The ethical standard reaches beyond an institution’s internal convenience. The AMA describes medicine as a moral activity directed toward care and alleviation of suffering, with duties of competence, communication, respect, patient welfare, and advocacy. It also calls on physicians to address external influences that undermine the patient–physician relationship. These are professional ethical commitments, not findings that any particular legal claim has been proved. [Source-review lead S30: AMA patient–physician relationships opinion; SOC-C118/C146, #139/#142.]

The moral question is whether the person remains the purpose of the arrangement or has become the resource consumed to keep the arrangement functioning.

My spiritual standard asks something further of me: to seek accountability without surrendering the humanity of the people whose conduct I challenge. I can demand an explanation without claiming to know another person’s soul. I can preserve evidence favorable to someone I accuse. I can seek protection for other patients without asking that a clinician be sacrificed to conceal a larger failure.

But that restraint must not be mistaken for permission to leave the failure unresolved.

The word *standard* has other meanings worth keeping. It can name a banner under which people assemble, a structure that provides support, or a musical composition that endures in a shared repertoire. [Source-review lead S31: lexical meanings and literary analogies; SOC-C024, #128.]

A banner announces what an organization stands for. A support is judged by what it can carry. A familiar composition still has to be performed; recognition of its name is not a substitute for playing it.

An institution may display its standards, publish them, teach them, and invoke them in correspondence. The patient encounters something more concrete: whether those standards hold when his communication is slow, his pain is difficult to describe, his history is disputed, and his case crosses the boundaries between departments.

That is where a claim to care becomes testable.

I am not asking that every uncertainty be resolved before treatment proceeds. I am asking that uncertainty be assigned, investigated where appropriate, and accompanied by a responsible plan.

I am not asking that the doctor become the lawyer. I am asking that the patient not be left to perform the uncoordinated work of both.

I am not asking to be relieved of agency. I am asking to be relieved of the institutional labor that has been confused with agency.

So I return to the question.

Is it standard for the patient to do all this?

If it is customary, examine the custom. If it is said to be necessary, identify the necessity. If it is said to be proportionate, account for the burden. If it is said to be unavoidable, show what alternatives were considered. If it is said to be care, show what care the work produced.

**Do not measure the adequacy of your institution by how much of its unfinished work a disabled patient can still perform.**

Measure it by whether the work is competently assigned, accessibly completed, and sustained without requiring the person seeking care to become the institution’s substitute.

I should not have to be extraordinary enough to obtain ordinary care.

That is the standard I am asking you to meet.
