## 1. Bedbound does not mean paralyzed

The first received clinical summary proposed three pillars: venous-thromboembolism prevention (mechanical compression, possible anticoagulants, movement), pressure management (a two-hour rule, dynamic mattresses, friction/shear reduction), and range-of-motion/physical and occupational therapy. It included consumer and root-domain references and follow-up questions about involuntary movement and equipment.

The assistant's first literary response, “What holds me here,” argued that a brief demonstration of standing must be recorded with its pain, duration, assistance, repeatability and recovery. The form is a means of eliciting information, not a limit on how extensive pain may be. The broad marking on a form is not itself a completed psychiatric assessment. Physical and psychiatric care should not be alternatives that erase one another. Preventive work and investigation/relief of pain are concurrent. The plan should identify what is attempted, who reviews results and what happens when the intervention cannot be tolerated.

The accompanying clinical corrections distinguished chronic outpatient immobility from acute inpatient prophylaxis, individualized repositioning from an inflexible two-hour rule, and pressure-redistributing support from a universally required alternating-pressure/low-air-loss mattress. These corrections require source rechecking under #130; neither the original advice nor the corrections become individualized prescriptions by being archived.

Source routing: #129, #130, #152–#154. Preserve the author's own distinction; never relabel the condition “total immobility” or “paralysis” for apparent precision.

## 3. Interdisciplinary treatment summary and the work between specialties

The next pasted summary asserted a highly coordinated neuropsychiatric/neuropalliative model, dual-purpose antidepressants, strict dopamine-blocker avoidance, restricted benzodiazepines, Somatic Experiencing or bedside therapy, co-scheduled rehabilitation and autonomy. It proposed a neuro-palliative team as the central hub and asked about care managers and home/telehealth specialists.

The assistant drafted “The work between the specialties.” Its central argument: specialists may divide work, but the patient should not be left to repair the divisions. Reconciliation, scheduling and follow-up must consider the patient's combined capacity and the effect of one treatment on the rest of life. Autonomy is not unassisted system management. A referral order is not demonstrated access. A support person's presence is not an institutional staffing substitute. Care setting must reflect the expressed preference for accessible in-person neurological evaluation, not a home/virtual default inferred from disability.

Corrections kept palliative care distinct from hospice and a universally mandatory specialist team. They distinguished established PTSD therapies from an unsupported claim that bedbound status makes Somatic Experiencing the preferred replacement. Duloxetine indications were separated from rigid-tone treatment. Parkinson-specific dopamine-blocker and pimavanserin discussion was not generalized to every movement disorder. Benzodiazepine cautions for PTSD were distinguished from possible neurological indications. These are evidence targets under #131/#132, not cleared treatment advice.

## 6. “Standard” as a sustained argument

The next user request broadened standard across its contexts and asked whether a patient should have to act as doctor, lawyer, advocate, researcher, archivist, accessibility designer, coordinator and investigator. The assistant produced the long essay “Standard,” attributed as a proposed literary draft for R.A. Jacob Martone. It is not an original signed statement, a legal determination or an author-accepted book chapter.

Its sequence is: what kind of standard; no standard patient; professions that arrive in the bed; standards the author already applied; reciprocal professional standards; what the institution does with burden; whether anyone asks; and standards that bear weight.

The distinctions include descriptive custom versus evaluative criteria; ordinary internal process versus professional care in actual circumstances; legal requirements versus ethical commitments and institutional representations; gold-standard benchmark versus universal mandate; uniformity versus effective communication; standard grammar versus credibility; calibration/traceability versus fitness of inference; proof thresholds for adjudication versus reasons to investigate; a double standard versus legitimately different role responsibilities; economic efficiency versus invisible transfer of work; standard of living versus mere survival; and the literary senses of banner, support and musical repertoire.

The central questions concern actual behavior and accountable responsibility, not unknown private motives. Preserve the institution's offered measures, legitimate professional boundaries, confidentiality, staff safety and contrary evidence. Do not convert a letter's conditions into a completed discharge, a policy into proof of compliance or violation, or allegations into convictions. No requirement that a patient be exceptional enough to obtain ordinary care.
