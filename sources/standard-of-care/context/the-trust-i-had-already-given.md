## The trust I had already given

I have given my trust to doctors for my entire life.

I did not begin by asking to be my own physician, lawyer, advocate, records custodian, and investigator. I began as a patient. I brought what I could describe to people whose knowledge I respected, trusting that they would help determine what it meant. I did not expect to supply the diagnosis before receiving an examination, identify every professional obligation before receiving its protection, or design an accessible clinical relationship before being allowed to participate in one.

**The history of this relationship must not begin at the moment I became unwilling to trust it without evidence. It must include the trust I had already given—and what happened while I was giving it.**

That trust was not an abstract endorsement of medicine. It concerned my body, my pain, my future, and the life I shared with my family. I relied on doctors to distinguish what could be explained from what remained uncertain, to tell me what they were doing, and to help me understand what should happen next. I accepted that medicine had limits. I did not understand those limits to mean that the work of reaching me could simply remain undone.

If there is a standard of patience, persistence, participation, and willingness to seek help that I was expected to meet, then the standard I have passed must be measured against the suffering through which I continued trying to obtain care. Not merely the number of appointments I attended or the documents I produced, but what it cost me to keep attending, explaining, waiting, and beginning again.

My endurance measures the burden I have borne. It does not certify the adequacy of the care I received.

Nor should suffering have been an examination I was required to pass. I did not become deserving of care only after enduring enough. The person who cannot write the next letter, withstand the next encounter, or reconstruct the next misunderstanding has not failed a legitimate test of patienthood.

Whatever investigation ultimately establishes about the causes of my physical deterioration, my minimum allegation remains: **a failure by the doctor responsible for my care to establish effective communication across the barriers I had identified, or to obtain the institutional help necessary to do so.** I have described a need extending beyond speech production to receiving questions, processing them, consolidating an answer, and completing its qualifications. Those needs cannot be answered merely by permitting me to bring a device while maintaining an encounter paced in a way that prevents its effective use. [Source-review lead T1: September 17 response, communication section; #138/#143.]

I am not asking a doctor to read my mind. I am asking what happened after I explained why the usual exchange was not reliably receiving it.

And those barriers were not placed there by me.

My neurological limitations were not rules I imposed on the clinic. My pain was not a condition I invented to obstruct examination. My need for time was not a refusal to answer. The difficulty existed; my request was for help communicating through it.

The institutional arrangements were not mine either. I did not divide responsibility among specialties. I did not determine how referrals would be received, how communication would be routed, or what would happen when an intended service could not be used. In my September 10 letter, I described returning to my referring neurologist after encountering disability-access and safety problems in the psychiatry pathway. I was asking for help with a failed route to care—not asking him to practice another specialty. [Source-review lead T2: September 10 letter, section 10, failed psychiatry pathway; #139.]

I encountered the barriers. I reported them. I tried to identify ways through them.

**The fact that I have had to describe a barrier does not make me its author.**

I have subsequently set protective limits. Those limits should be examined in their actual sequence: as responses to the conditions I reported, not as proof that I had never trusted doctors or never wanted care. Asking for explanation before touch, enough time to answer, or a reliable written route is an attempt to make participation possible. It should not be recast as an intention to prevent participation.

I have also expressly said that I do not require every mechanism I proposed to be adopted exactly as proposed. The underlying need is what I ask the institution to evaluate. That leaves room for a workable alternative; it does not leave room for treating the need itself as an obstacle of my making. [Source-review lead T3: September 17 response, accommodation section; #138.]

The same chronology matters when the institution encounters my present scrutiny. The writing, qualifications, evidence preservation, and requests for review did not arrive independently of the problems they address. I have already described how unresolved communication produces an incomplete history, how an incomplete history becomes a disputed record, and how that dispute produces further work for the patient. The work becomes visible in my hands. That does not establish that its cause began there. [Source-review lead T4: September 17 response, upstream burden section; #141.]

Before characterizing me as difficult to reassure, ask what reassurance was supported by action. Before treating my vigilance as the source of the difficulty, examine what made vigilance necessary.

There may be institutional barriers that constrained the doctor as well. I have expressly left that possibility open. If the physician lacked the authority, support, information, or effective means to obtain assistance, the investigation should follow those constraints rather than assign every failure to one individual. But an institutional obstacle to the physician’s work does not thereby become the patient’s responsibility to overcome. [Source-review lead T5: September 17 response, institutional constraint and investigation section; #139/#143.]

What, then, is an institution that claims to care doing when it leaves the patient to sustain both sides of the relationship—to keep offering the history and to construct the means by which that history might finally receive an answer?

Does anyone ask how much trust has already been spent sustaining that arrangement? Does anyone ask what it has cost the person who continued returning?

I do not know what anyone has considered privately. I ask what consideration became action.

Trust can be rebuilt through an explanation that addresses the question, a communication method that actually works, a responsibility that is accepted, and a commitment that is completed. It cannot be restored merely by making further trust another condition the patient must satisfy.

I have already demonstrated a lifetime of willingness to place my care in doctors’ hands. My present demand for an account does not erase that history. It asks what became of it.

**Do not ask me to prove my trust by enduring more of what has damaged it. Show me what you have done to make that trust possible again.**
