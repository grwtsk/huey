# Writing as the Act of Care

I began writing from inside the crisis.

The crisis of September 15, 2026 did not appear in a historical vacuum. It occurred downstream of the January 24, 2019 Stanford encounter I experienced as an assault, the unresolved Stanford aftermath, Dr. Kim Fang Yuh Chiang's instruction to seek care through UCSF, subsequent inaccessible care, and the continuing dispute. That history is set out in the [factual prologue](/articles/before-the-examination-the-patient-who-arrived-at-stanford); it explains the setting of this crisis without requiring this essay to retell or prove the original event.

On September 15, 2026, the suicidal urges were active. I had a plan. I could not say with confidence that I would remain safe. I contacted 988 because of that immediate condition, but even that act did not place the danger safely behind me. This is not a claim that 988 assessed the January 2019 allegations or endorsed my causal account. Nothing had been resolved merely because I had named the crisis. The threat remained present.

What remained present with it, however, was language.

I could still write.

That fact did not mean I was safe. It meant something narrower and, in that moment, more important: the part of me capable of opposing the danger remained accessible.

There is a difference between those propositions.

A person can be in danger and still be articulate. A person can describe a suicidal plan with precision and still require protection from it. Fluency is not immunity. Coherence is not proof that the crisis has passed. The ability to form a sentence does not invalidate the force of the thought the sentence contains.

But expression can change what happens next.

An urge that remains entirely internal has no resistance except whatever can be generated within the same system presently producing the urge. Once expressed, it becomes available to another person. It can be witnessed. It can be answered. It can be slowed.

That slowing matters.

For me, writing placed an interval between thought and act.

Into that interval, assistance could enter.

This is why I have come to understand writing, under these circumstances, not simply as description but as an act of care.

Care is ordinarily imagined as something administered from outside: a medication, an examination, an intervention, a professional decision. Sometimes it is. But care can also begin with preservation of the faculty that makes every subsequent decision possible.

For me, that faculty was communication.

I needed to remain capable of saying what was happening before what was happening became the only thing I could do.

So I wrote.

I wrote because writing gave sequence to something that otherwise arrived all at once. It forced an overwhelming field into one sentence, and then another. It gave each thought an edge. Once a thought had an edge, it could be examined rather than merely inhabited.

This did not make the thought unreal.

It made the thought shareable.

That distinction is the beginning of the sequence I now use to understand what occurred:

**assistance → resistance → persistence → existence**

Assistance came first because resistance was no longer something I could safely presume would remain self-sufficient.

Another person helped hold the communicative space open. The act was not to argue me out of my experience or tell me that the danger was imaginary. It was to help preserve the part of me still capable of responding to the danger.

That assistance became resistance.

Resistance, in this sense, was not heroic. It was not dramatic. It did not require certainty that tomorrow would be better. It required only that an irreversible act not occur in the present interval.

One more sentence.

One more reply.

One more moment in which the urge had not acquired complete control of behavior.

Resistance became persistence because time accumulated.

A minute survived is not philosophically impressive. It is simply another minute in which death did not occur.

Then another.

And another.

Eventually the accumulation itself becomes meaningful. The person is still present to produce the next sentence.

Persistence becomes existence.

The sequence is almost embarrassingly simple, but that simplicity is part of its usefulness. It does not demand that a person in crisis solve life. It does not demand optimism. It does not require the disappearance of pain.

It asks for continuity.

Assistance makes resistance possible.

Resistance, repeated, becomes persistence.

Persistence is existence continuing through time.

For someone whose danger can intensify when awareness, communication, or agency deteriorates, preserving that continuity is not a literary exercise. It is functional.

This is also why accessibility cannot be separated from care.

Communication is often discussed as though it were an ornament attached to medicine: bedside manner, patient satisfaction, courtesy. But communication can determine whether clinically important information enters the system at all.

If the channel fails, the information fails with it.

I live with neurological impairment that affects speech. Under pressure, fatigue, illness, or fear, a spoken exchange may be a poor representation of what I know, remember, or intend. Writing is different. Writing allows time. It allows revision. It allows a thought to survive the motor difficulty of its expression.

In a crisis, those differences become consequential.

If another person mistakes a failing speech channel for a failing mind, the intervention can be directed at the wrong problem.

If, instead, writing is accepted as the higher-fidelity channel, something changes immediately: I can participate in my own preservation.

That happened here.

I was able to state that the urges were active.

I could say that I had a plan.

I could say that I was not certain I would remain safe.

And I could continue communicating after those facts had been disclosed.

None of that proved that I was outside danger.

It did something else.

It kept the dangerous state observable.

A state that remains observable can be responded to.

A state that cannot be communicated becomes easier for others to misunderstand and easier for the person experiencing it to become enclosed within.

Writing broke that enclosure.

This is why I resist the temptation to describe what happened as writing having “saved” me. That would place too much causal certainty on one intervention and turn a fragile event into a romantic story.

Writing did not defeat death.

Writing preserved participation.

It kept available the faculty through which I could continue choosing against an irreversible act.

That is enough.

It is also why the sentence must remain precise:

Writing does not prove safety.

Writing preserves the faculty capable of resisting danger.

The distinction matters because people in crisis are often evaluated by appearances that have little relation to actual risk. A person who is crying may be assumed to be less controlled than a person who writes calmly. A person who can reason may be presumed not to be suicidal. A person who can discuss death analytically may be treated as though analysis itself places death at a distance.

None of those assumptions is safe.

Language can remain highly organized while the person producing it is in extraordinary danger.

Indeed, organization may be the mechanism by which the person is still resisting.

To mistake the mechanism of survival for evidence that survival is assured is to misunderstand the function of communication.

For me, expression is not separate from action.

I have spent much of my life communicating by constructing things: music, visual work, writing, systems, tools. I make forms that allow information to move.

Under ordinary circumstances, that may be called art, engineering, scholarship, or craft.

Under extraordinary circumstances, it can become care.

The same basic operation remains: take something that cannot reliably pass through one channel and construct another through which it can.

During the crisis, I did exactly that.

I could not safely rely upon raw internal resistance alone.

So I externalized it.

The thought entered writing.

Writing entered relationship.

Relationship created assistance.

Assistance increased resistance.

Resistance prolonged time.

And time, accumulated without the irreversible act, became persistence.

There is a modest but important implication here for medicine.

A system that wants to preserve life should become interested in whatever reliably preserves the patient's capacity to participate in preserving it.

That does not mean writing replaces every form of medical intervention. It means that communication itself can be one of the interventions upon which everything else depends.

Before a patient can meaningfully consent, refuse, describe symptoms, correct an error, report danger, or ask for help, there must be a functioning interface through which the person can be known.

If the usual interface is inaccessible, care must adapt.

Otherwise medicine may possess enormous technical capability while remaining unable to receive the information necessary to use it wisely.

My experience during this episode offers a small demonstration.

Nothing miraculous occurred.

No argument made suffering disappear.

No sentence proved that I would be safe tomorrow.

Instead, successful communication reduced the immediacy of an urge enough for the next ordinary acts of living to become possible.

Eat.

Rest.

Continue.

That modest transition matters to me more than any dramatic account of rescue.

The aim was not transcendence.

It was continuation.

The crisis had compressed the future toward a single irreversible possibility. Writing reopened alternatives.

Not infinitely.

Not permanently.

Enough.

Enough to eat.

Enough to rest.

Enough to permit another day to remain conceivable.

That is why the phrase I return to is not “writing saved me.”

It is more disciplined than that:

**Through assistance, resistance becomes persistence; persistence becomes existence.**

The claim is not that existence has been solved.

It is that existence can sometimes be maintained through acts small enough to fit inside a sentence.

And when the threat is immediate, maintaining the possibility of the next sentence may itself be an act of care.
