# January 24, 2019: The Origin Point

January 24, 2019 is the point at which the story changes.

Before that day, I was already neurologically impaired. I had balance problems. I had sensory changes. I had sought specialist care because something was wrong.

But I was still walking substantial distances. I was still working, traveling, participating in family life, and compensating for what my body was beginning to lose.

Then I met Dr. Steven Lee McIntire at Stanford.

I do not use the date because a date proves causation.

I use it because that is where the discontinuity in my experience begins.

During the encounter, I experienced Dr. McIntire's conduct as increasingly hostile and dismissive. I remember statements including, “Good for you,” “Talk about your shoulder,” “This is not a strep throat clinic,” and “You don't have MS.”

Those words did not occur in isolation from the reason I was there.

I was trying to explain neurological symptoms.

I was already having difficulty with balance.

When I attempted tandem walking—heel to toe—I could not perform it normally without reaching for the wall.

That observation matters to me because the medical record later characterized my gait and tandem gait as normal.

I dispute that characterization.

I also dispute documentation of coordination maneuvers that I say were not performed as recorded, including finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin testing.

Those disagreements are not peripheral to what followed. They concern whether the record accurately preserved what could be seen during the examination itself.

My wife was in the room.

She is therefore not merely someone who heard my account afterward. She witnessed the encounter.

That distinction matters.

She can speak to what she saw and heard during the examination. She can also speak, as my wife, to the person who entered the room and the person who left it.

At some point during the encounter, something in me changed sharply.

I remember saying:

**“Now, my legs are dead.”**

That sentence remains fixed in my memory because it was not a retrospective theory.

It was my reaction in the moment.

I experienced crushing bilateral leg pain. I experienced a profound change in my body. I experienced the encounter as no longer merely dismissive but violating.

I also describe physical contact during the encounter as nonconsensual.

I experienced that contact as an assault.

That is my statement.

It should neither be inflated into an adjudicated legal conclusion nor reduced to a meaningless expression of dissatisfaction.

Whether particular conduct by Dr. McIntire satisfies the elements of battery, medical battery, professional misconduct, or another legal offense is not something I can decide merely by writing it.

That question belongs to lawful authorities applying law to evidence.

But legal adjudication and lived experience answer different questions.

I can say that I experienced an assault.

My wife can say what she witnessed.

The medical record can be compared with our accounts.

The events that followed can be examined.

And a court, licensing authority, or other lawful body may separately determine what conclusions the evidence supports.

Those propositions can coexist without weakening one another.

What gives January 24 its importance is not simply the confrontation I remember.

It is what happened next.

Before the appointment, Apple Health recorded approximately 94.7 miles of walking during the preceding thirty-one days—about 3.06 miles and 7,128 steps per day.

That does not prove neurological normality.

It establishes substantial ambulatory function.

In the first month after the event, my recorded activity fell to approximately 1.11 miles and 2,699 steps per day.

By the final month of 2019, the corresponding figures were approximately 0.19 miles and 575 steps per day.

Those numbers establish a functional discontinuity.

They do not, standing alone, establish its mechanism.

That distinction is essential.

The observation is that my ambulatory activity declined dramatically across the period following January 24.

The causal inference is that something occurring during the McIntire encounter caused or accelerated that decline.

I make that causal claim.

I also recognize that causation must be tested rather than assumed merely because two events are temporally adjacent.

But an alternative account carries its own burden.

It must explain why a person who had walked almost ninety-five miles during the preceding month subsequently entered such a steep functional decline.

It must account for the contemporaneous symptoms.

It must account for the examination itself.

It must account for the disputed documentation.

It must account for the eyewitness.

It must account for what became visible afterward to clinicians and ordinary observers.

Temporal association is not causation.

But neither is temporal association nothing.

It tells us where to look.

January 24 also became an origin point for another reason.

I had already known trauma.

Long before Stanford, I had experienced childhood abuse, sexual violence, instability, poverty, homelessness, and failures of care within my own family.

Some of those events were extraordinarily serious.

But they had acquired a place in my history.

They had not disappeared.

Rather, through years of living, through relationships, through love, and through the complicated work of continuing to exist beside what had happened, they no longer governed my present in the same way.

This is difficult to explain without making trauma sound simpler than it is.

Love did not make earlier abuse acceptable.

It changed my relationship to its persistence.

I could love my father despite the ways in which I believe he failed me.

I could remember what had happened without being required to relive the same structure every time I entered a room.

The past had become, at least in part, past.

January 24 disrupted that settlement.

Something about the encounter reactivated a structure I already knew intimately: authority, vulnerability, disbelief, loss of agency, bodily violation, and the experience of being unable to make the person with power recognize what was happening.

That is a psychological observation about me.

It is not proof of Dr. McIntire's intent.

It is not proof that every detail of my interpretation is correct.

It establishes that the encounter became traumatic in a way that reorganized what came afterward.

From that point forward, medicine itself acquired a different emotional meaning.

The white coat was no longer simply associated with help.

Examination was no longer simply observation.

The medical record was no longer simply a neutral container of facts.

Authority could now mean something else.

That change matters because it affects everything that follows in the chronology.

When later clinicians encountered fear, distrust, difficulty communicating, resistance to particular forms of examination, or insistence upon written records, those behaviors did not arise from nowhere.

They had a history.

That history began, for me, on January 24.

This does not mean every later problem was caused by Dr. McIntire.

It means the encounter became one of the organizing events through which later care was experienced.

That is different.

A traumatic origin does not have to explain everything in order to explain something essential.

It can change the meaning of later events.

It can make abandonment more threatening.

It can make disputed records harder to tolerate.

It can make physical examination carry the memory of lost agency.

It can make silence from a physician feel like continuation rather than absence.

And it can make another institutional failure resemble the first.

That is why the response of Stanford after the encounter matters separately from the encounter itself.

If January 24 was the injury, what followed was the question of whether the institution would recognize, investigate, explain, correct, or repair what I said had happened.

My account is that it did not.

What I experienced instead was continued contradiction of my account and preservation of disputed clinical conclusions.

That later history deserves its own examination.

But it should not be allowed to obscure the original point.

There was a before.

There was an encounter.

There was an immediate reaction.

There was an eyewitness.

There was a marked decline afterward.

There was trauma.

Those statements do not require a completed legal adjudication in order to exist.

Nor does the absence of an adjudication erase them.

At the same time, I do not ask anyone to treat my causal attribution as self-proving.

The intellectually serious question is not whether one can imagine another explanation.

One always can.

The serious question is whether another explanation accounts for the same observations with comparable specificity.

What happened during the examination?

Why does my account differ from the record?

What did my wife observe?

What explains the abrupt symptoms I reported in the room?

What explains the functional trajectory that followed?

What explains the reactivation of trauma at precisely this point?

What explanation best accommodates the entire sequence without simply deleting the parts that are inconvenient?

That is the inquiry I am asking medicine to undertake.

January 24, 2019 is therefore not important because I have declared it important.

It is important because several independent lines of my history converge there.

My body changed.

My function changed.

My relationship to medicine changed.

My trauma changed.

The record became disputed.

And the questions generated that day remained alive long after the appointment ended.

I call it the origin point because that is what it became in my life.

Whether lawful authorities ultimately assign legal responsibility to Dr. Steven Lee McIntire is a question they must answer.

The existence of the event in my experience requires no such permission.

I lived it.

My wife was there.

And what came afterward must still be explained.
