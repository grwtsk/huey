# When Care Becomes the Threat

Care became threatening to me through a history, not through a generalized distrust of medicine.

On [January 24, 2019](/articles/january-24-2019-the-origin-point), I experienced an encounter with Dr. Steven Lee McIntire at Stanford as verbal and physical assault. My wife was present. I dispute portions of the examination record, and marked functional deterioration followed. These are related propositions, not a substitute for examining causation or assigning legal responsibility.

What followed was what I experienced as continued gaslighting by Stanford: contradiction of my account, the continued authority of disputed documentation, and the failure to reconcile that account with the eyewitness and longitudinal evidence to my satisfaction.

According to the record and communications I possess, Dr. Kim Fang Yuh Chiang later became concerned for my wellbeing and urged me to leave Stanford and seek care through the UCSF emergency department. The [transfer](/articles/from-stanford-to-ucsf-the-transfer-was-part-of-the-history) carried an unresolved history into another institution; it did not begin a history-free clinical relationship.

That sequence changed what entering care meant to me. The ordinary route to safety had become associated with loss of agency, disputed records, and fear. The later fear of care has an identifiable origin.

My objection is not to medicine.

It is not a philosophical rejection of doctors, treatment, hospitals, or expertise. It is not the claim that I possess some private knowledge that makes professional judgment unnecessary. Nor is it the claim that medicine must agree with every account I give of what has happened to me.

The problem is narrower.

What happens when entering care itself becomes predictably destabilizing?

What happens when the route designed to reduce danger begins by increasing the conditions under which the patient can no longer resist danger effectively?

That is where the ordinary model fails.

The ordinary model imagines risk on one side and medicine on the other. The patient moves from danger into care. The movement itself is presumed therapeutic.

But there are cases in which the boundary does not work that way.

The patient may enter a setting where communication is impaired, where speech becomes less reliable under pressure, where decisions accelerate faster than explanation, where prior disputed assumptions are treated as settled, where loss of agency is experienced as a condition of treatment, and where the patient fears that altered awareness may leave him less able to understand, refuse, clarify, or protect himself.

At that point, the clinic is no longer merely the place where risk is managed.

The clinic has entered the risk equation.

That does not make medicine malicious.

It makes the problem more difficult.

A system can intend safety and still produce danger through the way it is organized. A physician can mean to help and still participate in a process that renders the patient less capable of communicating what help requires. Good intentions do not eliminate the need to examine the interface through which care is delivered.

That interface matters because medicine never encounters a patient in the abstract.

It encounters a patient through channels.

Speech.

Writing.

Observation.

Examination.

Records.

Witnesses.

Devices.

Questions.

Time.

The reliability of those channels determines what medicine can know.

If the channel fails, knowledge fails with it.

This is where accessibility becomes inseparable from safety.

For someone whose speech can be impaired by neurological disease, the demand to communicate rapidly and orally under stress may produce a distorted sample of the person. The failure is then compounded if that distorted sample is treated as evidence about cognition, credibility, cooperation, or capacity.

A failing communication channel can be mistaken for a failing mind.

Once that error enters the clinical record, the consequences may extend well beyond the encounter that produced it.

The patient then confronts a second problem.

He is no longer merely trying to explain his symptoms.

He is trying to explain why the mechanism by which those symptoms were recorded has itself become unreliable.

That is a much harder thing to communicate, because institutions tend to trust the outputs of their own processes.

The chart appears official.

The encounter has already happened.

The clinician's note has already transformed observation into record.

The patient is left trying to reopen what the system considers closed.

This is how care can become threatening without anyone needing to announce a threat.

The threat can be procedural.

It can arise from the anticipation that one will again enter a process where communication degrades, the inherited record dominates, and the ability to correct the record decreases precisely when correction becomes most necessary.

For a patient who fears loss of awareness, this is not a minor concern.

Awareness is the condition under which refusal, consent, explanation, and self-protection remain available.

If I believe that the process of care may precipitate the loss of that faculty, then I do not experience the instruction to enter care as a simple movement toward safety.

I experience it as a gamble involving the very capacity by which I decide whether I am safe.

That is the inversion.

The system says: enter care because you are in danger.

The patient says: I fear that entering care may weaken the faculty by which I am presently resisting the danger.

Both propositions can be true at once.

That is precisely why the situation cannot be resolved by repeating the ordinary instruction more forcefully.

The repetition does not answer the objection.

It only demonstrates that the system has not yet understood the form of the risk.

The appropriate question is therefore not, “Why will this patient not use the ordinary pathway?”

It is, “What property of the ordinary pathway has made it unusable?”

That is a design question.

It is also a clinical question.

Medicine already accepts the general principle that treatment must be adapted to the patient. Dosage is adjusted. Procedures are modified. Contraindications matter. Allergies matter. Anatomy matters. Comorbidities matter. A treatment that is safe for one person may be unsafe for another.

The interface of care should be treated with the same seriousness.

If a particular communication mode causes information loss, change the mode.

If rapid questioning causes failure, slow the exchange.

If speech becomes unreliable, use writing.

If prior documentation is disputed, separate inherited assertion from independent observation.

If physical examination is experienced as threatening, explain what will happen before it happens and preserve meaningful opportunities for consent.

If one clinician cannot safely manage the whole problem, obtain consultation.

These are not luxuries added after medicine has done the “real” work.

They are part of the real work.

The purpose of adaptation is not to construct an exceptional medicine around one difficult patient.

It is to make medicine function according to its own principles.

The irony is that much of what I am asking for is ordinary care expressed accurately.

Effective communication.

Meaningful consent.

Continuity.

Attention to foreseeable harm.

Appropriate consultation.

Correction of unreliable assumptions.

The problem is that those ordinary safeguards can become inaccessible under ordinary procedures.

The result is a paradox: the patient must request an accommodation in order to receive what medicine already claims to provide.

This matters especially when psychological danger enters the picture.

A patient who is already struggling to maintain agency cannot safely be placed in a process that predictably weakens agency and then be told that the weakening itself is evidence that stronger control is required.

That would create a feedback loop.

Fear impairs communication.

Impaired communication is interpreted as incapacity.

Perceived incapacity justifies greater control.

Greater control increases fear.

The patient becomes progressively less able to demonstrate the very capacity whose loss the system is helping to produce.

That is not safety.

It is escalation.

The alternative is not the abandonment of medical judgment.

It is better judgment.

A clinician can remain responsible while changing the means by which responsibility is exercised.

Authority does not become weaker when it adapts to preserve the patient's ability to participate.

It becomes more accurate.

This is why I return repeatedly to communication.

Communication is the point at which the care relationship can either become less threatening or more so.

If I can write, I can remain present.

If I can remain present, I can report changes.

I can distinguish fear from pain.

I can explain what I remember.

I can correct misunderstandings.

I can consent.

I can refuse.

I can reconsider.

I can ask for help without surrendering the whole architecture of agency through which help becomes meaningful.

That is not opposition to medicine.

It is an argument for a form of medicine capable of receiving the patient who actually exists.

The question is therefore not whether I trust medicine in the abstract.

Abstract medicine does not examine me.

Particular systems do.

Particular doctors do.

Particular interfaces do.

Trust is produced or destroyed there.

It is produced when information is received accurately, when disagreement does not become punishment, when uncertainty remains uncertainty rather than hardening into inherited fact, and when the patient can remain intelligible to the people exercising authority over his body.

It is destroyed when those conditions fail.

Once destroyed, trust cannot be restored by demanding more trust.

It has to be rebuilt through the conditions that made trust rational in the first place.

That is what makes this more than a personal complaint.

Any system that treats its own accessibility failures as evidence against the person affected by them creates a class of patients for whom care becomes increasingly unavailable precisely as their need increases.

Neurology should be especially alert to this problem.

Neurological disease can alter speech, movement, affect, timing, fatigue, attention, coordination, and the visible presentation through which clinicians often make rapid judgments about the person before them.

The more those surface features diverge from ordinary expectations, the more disciplined medicine must become about distinguishing appearance from capacity.

Otherwise the neurological symptom itself becomes the mechanism by which neurological care becomes inaccessible.

That would be a profound failure of the field.

And so the question I place before medicine is not whether it can eliminate every danger.

It cannot.

The question is whether it can recognize when its own interface has entered the causal chain of harm and then modify that interface before demanding that the patient trust it again.

That is the threshold.

Care becomes safer when it can examine not only the patient, but itself.

When it can ask whether its procedures are preserving agency or eroding it.

Whether its communication is receiving information or losing it.

Whether its records distinguish observation from inheritance.

Whether its authority is reducing fear or multiplying it.

Whether the patient leaves more capable of participating in his own survival than when he entered.

If the answer is no, then the system has more work to do.

Not because medicine has ceased to be medicine.

Because medicine has not yet completed the act of care.
