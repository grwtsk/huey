# Before the Examination: The Patient Who Arrived at Stanford

Before asking what happened on January 24, 2019, I need to establish something simpler: who arrived at Stanford that day.

This is a baseline question.

It does not determine what caused what came afterward. It does not prove intent. It does not decide whether a particular act caused a particular neurological consequence. Its purpose is narrower: to establish the condition, function, observable manifestations, and available evidence that existed before Dr. Steven Lee McIntire examined me.

Without that denominator, every later comparison becomes unstable.

I did not arrive at Stanford as a neurologically ordinary person.

There were already manifestations of neurological disease. Balance was impaired. My perception of sound had changed. Difficulties that would become important during the examination were not inventions produced afterward. When I was asked to walk heel-to-toe, I could not perform the task normally without using the wall for support. I experienced myself as unsteady. These were observable motor phenomena, not conclusions about their cause.

That distinction is important.

The observation is that balance difficulty existed and could be seen.

The inference is what disease, lesion, mechanism, or combination of mechanisms accounted for it.

Those are different propositions.

My argument does not require them to be collapsed.

Nor was neurological impairment incompatible with substantial function.

Immediately before the examination, I remained unusually active for someone living with neurological disease. My Apple Health data provide one quantitative view of that function.

For the thirty-one days ending January 24, 2019, Apple Health recorded approximately **94.7 miles of walking**, an average of about **3.06 miles and 7,128 steps per day**.

Across the broader pre-event annual baseline, the corresponding averages were approximately **2.27 miles and 5,279 steps per day**.

Those two numbers should not be confused.

The thirty-one-day figure describes my immediately preceding level of activity.

The annual figure provides a broader comparison and shows that the month immediately before the Stanford examination was not an anomalously inactive period being mistaken for normal health. I was walking more during that immediate month than my already substantial annual average.

Apple Health does not tell us why I walked.

It does not measure cerebellar function directly.

It does not prove the quality of every step, exclude pain or imbalance, or establish that I had no neurological impairment.

It establishes something much more modest and more useful: before January 24, I was producing a sustained quantity of ambulatory activity that can be compared with what came later.

That is the limit of the measurement, and it is also its value.

The same distinction applies to the rest of the baseline.

Before the examination, I was traveling, working, participating in family life, and walking substantial distances. I had traveled with my wife and children and had taken walking tours through cities such as Paris and London while carrying a pack.

None of that proves normal neurology.

A person can walk miles and still have cerebellar disease.

A person can work and still have impaired balance.

A person can compensate for neurological dysfunction for years before those compensations fail.

The relevant observation is therefore not that I was neurologically normal.

I was not.

The relevant observation is that I was neurologically impaired and nevertheless functionally active.

That is the patient who arrived.

This matters because one of the easiest errors in reconstructing what happened afterward would be to force the baseline into one of two false categories: either I was healthy before January 24, or I was already so impaired that subsequent deterioration carries little evidentiary significance.

Neither accurately describes the evidence I have.

The baseline was mixed.

There was disease.

There was visible impairment.

There was also substantial preserved function.

Those facts can coexist.

Indeed, that combination is precisely what makes the later trajectory worth examining.

## What was observable

Some of the evidence is behavioral.

People who knew me could see how I moved through ordinary life. They could see whether I worked, traveled, walked through neighborhoods, participated in family activities, climbed stairs, carried things, drove, stood, spoke, and navigated the environment.

They were not performing neurological examinations.

That is an important limitation.

But neurological examination does not have a monopoly on observable function.

A teacher does not need neurological training to know whether a student who previously walked into a classroom can still do so.

A neighbor does not need a medical license to notice a dramatic change in how someone crosses a driveway.

A spouse does not require specialist training to know whether her husband can walk through an airport, accompany his family through a city, or remain standing without reaching for support.

Such observations cannot determine etiology.

They can establish change.

That distinction should remain intact throughout this record.

## My wife occupies a different evidentiary position

My wife is both a longitudinal witness and an eyewitness to the January 24 encounter.

Those roles should not be conflated.

As a longitudinal witness, she knew my function before the appointment and observed what happened afterward.

As an eyewitness, she was physically present during the encounter with Dr. McIntire.

That gives her testimony a different evidentiary relationship to the examination itself than the testimony of friends, relatives, colleagues, former teachers, neighbors, or others who knew me across time but were not inside the examination room.

The latter witnesses cannot say what McIntire said or did.

They can say what they observed of me before and after.

That is still important evidence, but it answers a different question.

A rigorous account should preserve that distinction rather than treating all witnesses as interchangeable.

## The longitudinal witnesses

There are many people distributed across different periods of my life who knew what I looked like and how I functioned.

Family members knew me intimately.

Friends saw me socially.

Colleagues knew how I functioned professionally.

Teachers knew me before the events at issue.

Neighbors encountered me in ordinary daily activity.

Their usefulness is not that they collectively become a neurological examination by majority vote.

They do not.

Their usefulness is that longitudinal observation samples a human being across places and time in a way that a brief clinic examination cannot.

If many independent observers describe a marked functional discontinuity across the same period, that does not by itself establish its cause.

It does make the discontinuity harder to reduce to an artifact of a single appointment.

This is why the witness evidence and the quantitative evidence complement each other without becoming the same kind of evidence.

Apple Health records movement.

Witnesses observe function.

Clinical records contain professional observations and interpretations.

Contemporaneous writing records what I was reporting or thinking at particular times.

Photographs or video, where dated and relevant, can preserve visible function.

No individual category answers every question.

Together, they permit reconstruction.

## The disputed examination record

The January 24 examination itself contains another distinction that has to be preserved carefully.

Dr. McIntire's record reportedly describes findings including **normal gait and station**, heel-and-toe walking, **normal tandem gait**, a negative Romberg, and no dysmetria on finger-to-nose or heel-to-shin testing.

I dispute portions of that record.

Most concretely, I dispute the characterization of tandem walking as normal. My account is that I could not walk heel-to-toe without using the wall for support.

I also dispute the documentation of coordination testing that I say was not performed as recorded, including finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin testing.

Those are disputes about what occurred during an examination.

They should remain identified as disputes.

The chart is evidence that McIntire documented those findings.

My account is evidence that I experienced the examination differently and that some recorded maneuvers were not performed as charted.

My wife's presence matters because she provides an eyewitness independent of both the clinician who wrote the record and the patient challenging it.

The correct response is not to convert either side automatically into fact by status alone.

The correct response is to identify the disagreement and examine what other evidence can resolve or constrain it.

That is especially important because some manifestations were observable without sophisticated interpretation.

Whether a particular balance abnormality localizes to cerebellum, vestibular system, sensory pathways, medication effect, functional compensation, or some combination may require expertise.

Whether a person touched the wall while attempting tandem gait is a different kind of observation.

Neurological training determines how much can be inferred from the behavior.

It is not required merely to see the behavior.

## The baseline does not prove causation

This article deliberately stops short of the causal claim.

A strong pre-event functional baseline followed by marked deterioration can establish temporal discontinuity.

It cannot, by itself, identify the mechanism that produced that discontinuity.

Likewise, evidence that neurological impairment was visible before the appointment can bear upon what a clinician reasonably could have observed, but it does not by itself establish what that clinician actually perceived, understood, intended, or ignored.

Those are later questions.

The baseline establishes the starting state.

That is all it needs to do.

It also prevents a later argument from being built upon a patient who never existed.

The patient who arrived at Stanford was not neurologically normal.

He was not functionally destroyed.

He was living between those categories.

He had neurological manifestations serious enough to seek specialist care and visible enough to become relevant during examination.

At the same time, immediately before that appointment, Apple Health recorded roughly 94.7 miles of walking over thirty-one days—about 3.06 miles and 7,128 steps per day—against a broader pre-event annual average of approximately 2.27 miles and 5,279 steps per day.

He traveled.

He worked.

He participated in his family.

He compensated.

He was observed over time by people who knew what ordinary function looked like for him.

That is the denominator.

From there, the inquiry can move forward.

What changed after January 24 is a separate question.

Whether the change was caused by something that occurred during that encounter is another.

Whether the examination record accurately represents what happened inside the room is another still.

Whether subsequent institutional responses adequately confronted those questions belongs later in the chronology.

But none of those questions can be examined responsibly if the starting point is allowed to drift.

My baseline evidence does not tell the reader what conclusion to reach about January 24.

It tells the reader what must be accounted for.

A neurologically impaired but substantially ambulatory person entered Stanford.

The later record must begin there.
