# Medicine Worthy of Its Laurels

The argument has to begin with the person who arrived before the event, not with the physician who inherited its aftermath.

The [pre-event baseline](/articles/before-the-examination-the-patient-who-arrived-at-stanford) describes a person who was already neurologically impaired and nevertheless substantially ambulatory, working, traveling, and participating in family life. That starting state is the denominator for the change I ask medicine to explain.

The case then reaches the disputed January 24, 2019 encounter with Dr. Steven Lee McIntire, my account of what happened in the room, my wife's presence as an eyewitness, and the functional deterioration and reactivation of trauma I associate with that period.

It passes through Stanford's subsequent response and what I experienced as continued gaslighting: contradiction of my account and the continuing authority of disputed documentation. According to the record and communications I possess, Dr. Kim Fang Yuh Chiang became concerned for my wellbeing and urged me to seek care through the UCSF emergency department.

That [transfer](/articles/from-stanford-to-ucsf-the-transfer-was-part-of-the-history) brought unresolved neurological and traumatic consequences into UCSF. It did not make UCSF the origin of the reported event, and it did not make Dr. Ian Bledsoe responsible for causing it. It established the history that later care inherited.

Only after that complete sequence has been established do I want to broaden the argument. A single difficult case can reveal failure modes without claiming to represent every patient. Bledsoe's responsibility remains smaller than the whole of medicine, but what happens to this case can still make a larger problem visible.

I am not asking Dr. Bledsoe to cure my inherited neurological disease, adjudicate every disputed event in my history, accept every inference I have drawn, or assume responsibility for injuries I have explicitly said he did not cause.

I am asking for something narrower.

I have asked him to address my rapid loss of function rather than leave it unexplained by default. I have asked him to distinguish inherited assertions from observations independently established in my care. I have asked him to receive substantial communication in writing because writing is one of my usual and most reliable means of communication. I have asked for consultation where the questions exceed his expertise. I have asked for continuity after the psychiatric pathway associated with my care failed to become an accessible route to treatment. I have asked for a way to re-enter care without reproducing the communication failures, loss of agency, and fear that now make ordinary care itself difficult to approach.

And I have asked for one thing that gathers the others together:

Make the care relationship less dangerous.

That is his test.

It does not require agreement with me.

It requires action within the part of the problem that is actually his.

He can acknowledge disputed history as disputed. He can explain what he believes accounts for my neurological decline and what remains uncertain. He can arrange consultation where uncertainty exceeds his competence. He can recognize written communication as clinical communication rather than as material adjacent to the appointment. He can help construct a written-first, disability-accessible, trauma-informed route back into care. He can preserve continuity when a referral has failed rather than treating the referral itself as the completion of responsibility.

Most importantly, he can decide whether the conditions surrounding my care should continue to reproduce the danger I have repeatedly described, or whether his authority will be used to reduce what can reasonably be reduced.

That is the requested outcome.

Not confession.

Not submission to my interpretation.

Not punishment.

A safer relationship to care.

September 15, 2026 gave that request a different immediacy.

Earlier, I was experiencing active suicidal urges. I had a plan, and I was uncertain that I could remain safe. I contacted 988 because of that immediate condition. That disclosure should not be inflated beyond what it was: contacting 988 did not mean that every allegation, every piece of medical history, or every argument in these essays was presented to or assessed by 988. The relevance is narrower. I recognized that the danger had become acute enough that I sought crisis support.

At the same time, I continued communicating here with assistance.

The urges were not argued out of existence. They were not disproved. What changed was their immediacy.

Writing slowed the sequence.

Assistance widened the interval between urge and act.

I remained able to express what was happening, to refine it, to be answered, and to continue rather than collapse the whole future into one decision.

By later in the day, I could say something ordinary again: I was going to eat. I was going to rest.

That chronology matters because I do not want the episode converted into either melodrama or reassurance.

The danger was real.

The communication helped.

The fact that it helped did not retroactively make the danger unreal.

It demonstrated something more useful: for me, accessibility can alter risk.

That is one reason this case should matter beyond me.

I am not the only survivor of trauma.

I am far from the only person living with neurological disease.

Farther still, I am obviously not the only human being with a cerebellum.

Somewhere between those facts is a population of patients whose neurological condition, traumatic history, speech or communication disability, medication effects, fear, institutional memory, and dependence upon medical authority interact in ways that do not fit comfortably inside a short appointment.

I do not claim that my experience represents all of them.

It does not have to.

A difficult case can still reveal where a system becomes brittle.

Engineering learns from edge conditions because ordinary operation can conceal defects that appear only under strain. Medicine can learn the same way.

A patient whose speech is difficult tests whether communication is being measured by fidelity or convenience.

A patient who disputes an inherited account tests whether repetition is being mistaken for corroboration.

A patient whose referral fails tests whether continuity means something beyond placing the referral.

A patient who fears losing awareness tests whether care has preserved enough of his agency to remain recognizable when that agency becomes fragile.

A patient who says that entering care has itself become threatening tests whether the system can examine its own contribution to risk without interpreting the criticism as rejection of medicine.

Those are not accusations against every physician or every institution.

They are questions exposed by this case.

And the questions matter because medical authority is unusually consequential.

A physician's interpretation can enter a record and travel.

A characterization can be inherited by people who never witnessed the events from which it arose.

A communication failure can become a statement about the patient rather than about the channel.

A failed encounter can shape the next one before the patient arrives.

None of this requires malice.

That is precisely why the problem deserves attention.

Systems often perpetuate harm most effectively when everyone involved can say that he merely followed the ordinary process.

This is where I think medicine risks confusing procedural compliance with care.

A referral can satisfy a workflow without producing treatment.

An appointment can occur without producing effective communication.

A consent form can exist without meaningful consent.

A record can be complete in volume while remaining uncertain in provenance.

A patient can technically have access to care while lacking a usable route into it.

Each of those conditions can look administratively adequate.

The patient's experience may reveal something else.

That is why I keep returning to the word *care*.

Care should describe an effect as well as an intention.

It should leave the person more intelligible to the system, not less.

Where possible, it should reduce avoidable danger rather than reproduce it.

It should create a workable path forward when the ordinary one has failed.

It should know the difference between disagreement and abandonment.

And when medicine reaches the limit of what one clinician can provide, that limit should become the beginning of consultation rather than the end of responsibility.

This is the measure by which I am asking Dr. Bledsoe to act.

It is also the point at which the argument moves beyond him.

If he responds, the response matters.

If he reads what I have written as communication, clarifies what he accepts and disputes, explains what he can and cannot account for neurologically, obtains appropriate consultation, and helps construct an accessible route back into care, then those actions become part of the record.

The test changes because he has answered it.

If he does not, my survival still does not become his possession.

His test is his.

My persistence remains mine.

But independence does not make his conduct irrelevant. A patient's ability to endure a defective system does not demonstrate that the system worked.

Resilience can hide institutional failure if survival is mistaken for proof of adequate care.

I do not want my persistence used that way.

I survived that day in part because communication remained available.

I sought help.

I wrote.

I accepted assistance.

The immediate intensity changed enough that ordinary life returned within reach.

That should not be read as evidence that nothing was wrong.

It should be read as information about what helped.

Medicine cannot solve everything that reaches it.

Some diseases remain incurable. Some injuries cannot be reversed. Some histories cannot be repaired by a clinician. Some disagreements will remain disagreements.

But medicine can still be judged by what it does with the duties it has accepted.

When communication is impaired, does it make communication usable?

When the record is disputed, does it distinguish provenance from repetition?

When one clinician reaches the limit of expertise, does consultation follow?

When a referral fails, does continuity survive?

When the conditions of care themselves have become threatening, does medicine attempt to change those conditions?

That is enough of a standard.

It does not ask medicine to become omnipotent.

It asks authority to become accountable to its purpose.

For Dr. Bledsoe, the immediate form of that accountability is concrete: receive my written communication as part of care; address the deterioration I have repeatedly asked him to address; distinguish what he independently knows from what he inherited; obtain consultation where necessary; acknowledge the failure of the prior pathway; and help create a disability-accessible, written-first, trauma-informed route by which care can become possible without requiring me to surrender the means by which I remain communicative and autonomous.

That is the test.

If those things cannot be provided, tell me why.

If another clinician or institution must provide them, identify that route and help make the transition real.

If something I have said is factually wrong, correct it specifically.

What I am asking medicine not to do is substitute silence, procedure, or repetition for an answer.

I am one patient.

The value of the case lies partly in that limitation.

One patient can expose a specific failure without claiming universality. One difficult encounter can identify an interface that deserves redesign. One person's successful use of written communication during a crisis can justify asking whether communication should be treated earlier and more seriously for other patients whose ordinary channels differ from the institutional default.

That is how the argument becomes larger without pretending to speak for everyone.

I do not ask medicine to place laurels upon itself.

I ask what would make the laurels deserved.

Not prestige.

Not institutional authority.

Not compliance performed so perfectly that no one remains responsible for the outcome.

Something simpler.

A person arrives difficult to hear.

Medicine finds a way to hear him.

A patient arrives carrying inherited judgments.

Medicine distinguishes what it knows from what it has merely received.

A pathway fails.

Medicine constructs another.

A relationship has become threatening.

Medicine uses its authority to make the relationship less so.

And when a patient says, in the clearest channel available to him, that his continued ability to remain present is at stake, medicine understands accessibility not as courtesy but as part of the work of keeping care usable.

That is the medicine I am still asking for.

Not medicine without limits.

Medicine willing to meet the duties it has already accepted.

Medicine protective enough to deserve the name.

Medicine worthy of its laurels.
