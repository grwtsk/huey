# From Stanford to UCSF: The Transfer Was Part of the History

UCSF did not enter my history because I casually decided to seek another neurological opinion.

It entered later, after the January 24, 2019 examination with Dr. Steven Lee McIntire, after the functional deterioration I associate temporally with that encounter, and after my attempts to obtain an adequate response within Stanford had failed to produce one.

That chronology matters.

The first fact is the encounter itself. I have described what I experienced there as verbal and physical assault. My wife was present. The examination record contains findings I dispute. A marked decline in my ambulatory function followed.

Those propositions do not, by themselves, establish the complete causal explanation for what happened to me.

They establish the problem with which I remained.

The next part of the history concerns what Stanford did with that problem.

I continued trying to obtain care within the institution. I continued reporting that something significant had happened, that my function had changed, and that the record did not accurately represent portions of the examination as I remembered it.

The institutional response did not resolve those disputes.

This is what I mean when I refer to **what I experienced as continued gaslighting by Stanford**.

I do not use the word as a substitute for evidence or as a diagnosis of institutional intent. I use it to describe the effect of a particular pattern: my account remained contradicted; disputed documentation remained authoritative; the difference between my recollection and the chart was not reconciled to my satisfaction; and the significance of my wife's eyewitness account and the broader longitudinal evidence did not produce the kind of reconsideration I was seeking.

Those are the concrete matters underneath the word.

Whether individual clinicians believed they were acting reasonably is a separate question.

Whether Stanford deliberately intended to make me doubt my own experience is another.

I do not need to resolve either proposition in order to describe the result: I increasingly experienced the institution as preserving an account of events that I believed was inconsistent with what had happened to me.

That distinction between observation and interpretation should remain clear.

The observation is that the disputed record remained.

The observation is that I continued objecting to it.

The observation is that I continued deteriorating and seeking answers.

The interpretation is that the institutional response amounted to gaslighting.

That is why I qualify the term as my experience rather than asking the word itself to establish motive.

Then Dr. Kim Fang Yuh Chiang became important.

Dr. Chiang was my physician within Stanford.

Her role in this history should be described precisely because she was not merely an outside commentator on a dispute between patient and institution.

According to the record and communications I possess, she became concerned about my wellbeing and about the inability of Stanford to provide the support she believed I needed. She urged me to leave Stanford and seek care through the UCSF emergency department.

That is the role I attribute to her.

I do not mean that Dr. Chiang adjudicated my allegations against Dr. McIntire.

I do not mean that she formally declared Stanford responsible for the deterioration I was experiencing.

I do not mean that she established the neurological mechanism connecting January 24 to what followed.

Her action carries a narrower meaning.

My Stanford physician became sufficiently concerned about my condition and the adequacy of the situation within Stanford that she directed me outside her own institution for further care.

That is clinically important provenance.

It changes the meaning of my arrival at UCSF.

Without that history, the move can appear to be ordinary medical consumer behavior: a dissatisfied patient leaves one health system and seeks another opinion.

That is not how I experienced it, and it is not how I understand the sequence reflected in my record.

I was not simply looking for a different answer.

I was being directed away from an institution in which the problem had become unresolved enough that my own physician was concerned for my wellbeing.

The distinction matters because a receiving institution inherits more than a diagnosis.

It inherits the circumstances under which the patient arrived.

When I reached UCSF, I carried my neurological disease with me.

I also carried the reported January 2019 event.

I carried the disputed Stanford documentation.

I carried the functional deterioration that had followed.

I carried my wife's eyewitness account.

I carried the failure to obtain an explanation I found adequate.

And I carried a changing relationship to medicine itself.

Those conditions were already present before Dr. Ian Bledsoe became part of my care.

That does not make UCSF responsible for their origin.

It does mean that they formed part of the patient UCSF received.

This distinction is important because otherwise later distrust can appear irrationally self-generated.

If the history begins at UCSF, the patient can look unusually suspicious of clinicians, unusually insistent upon written records, unusually sensitive to physical examination, or unusually resistant to ordinary pathways of care.

If the history begins where it actually began for me, those same behaviors acquire context.

I had already experienced a neurological examination as traumatic.

I already believed that important parts of that examination had been documented inaccurately.

I already believed that my subsequent deterioration had not been adequately explained.

I had already spent time trying to make an institution reconsider its own record.

And then one of my physicians inside that institution told me, in substance, that I needed to go elsewhere for help.

That does not prove that all of my interpretations are correct.

It does explain why the next clinical relationship could not reasonably begin as though none of this had happened.

Trust was no longer an initial condition.

It had become a clinical variable.

That is the central importance of the Stanford-to-UCSF transfer.

The transfer itself did not resolve the original dispute.

It transported the unresolved dispute into another system.

And with it came a patient whose expectations of medicine had already changed.

I still wanted care.

That point is easy to lose.

Leaving Stanford did not represent rejection of medicine.

Going to the UCSF emergency department represented another attempt to obtain it.

The distinction matters because later reluctance toward particular forms of care can otherwise be misread as a generalized refusal of treatment.

My history suggests something different.

I continued seeking help even after my confidence in the mechanisms of care had been damaged.

I continued because I still believed medicine might be capable of understanding what had happened.

That expectation became part of what UCSF inherited too.

A receiving institution therefore faced two related tasks.

The first was neurological: evaluate the disease, the functional decline, and the questions still unresolved.

The second was relational: provide care to someone for whom the process of receiving care had itself acquired traumatic meaning.

Those tasks cannot be completely separated.

Communication affects examination.

Trust affects disclosure.

Fear affects whether a patient can remain present during an encounter.

Speech impairment affects what information a clinician receives.

Prior disputed documentation affects whether the patient believes new documentation will preserve his account faithfully.

The history travels through the interface.

This is why I resist describing the Stanford-to-UCSF movement merely as a transfer of care.

A transfer usually implies that responsibility passes from one functioning structure to another.

What happened here, as I experienced it, was closer to a transfer of an unresolved problem.

Stanford did not cease to matter because I crossed an institutional boundary.

The origin remained part of the case.

At the same time, the distinction between institutions remained morally important.

UCSF did not have to reproduce Stanford's failures.

Dr. Bledsoe did not have to adopt Dr. McIntire's conclusions.

A receiving physician had the possibility of examining the history independently.

That possibility is one reason the subsequent relationship matters so much to me.

I arrived with unresolved harm, but not with a predetermined conclusion about every physician who would come afterward.

A different institution could have produced a different experience.

A different neurologist could have created a different relationship to the record.

That remains the standard by which the later history should be read.

The question is not whether UCSF caused what happened at Stanford.

It did not.

The question is what UCSF did with what arrived.

And the question for Dr. Bledsoe is not whether he accepts legal responsibility for Dr. McIntire.

He should not be required to.

His responsibility begins with what was placed before him: the neurological deterioration, the disputed history, the communication disability, the failed prior pathways, and the patient's need for care that would not simply reproduce the conditions already experienced as threatening.

That is why provenance matters.

A patient does not become ahistorical when he enters a new hospital.

Neither does trauma.

The body that arrived at UCSF was the same body that had entered Stanford before the origin point and left it afterward.

The person was carrying both.

This is also where the next part of the series begins.

Because once care has become associated with loss of agency, contradiction, and fear, seeking help during a later crisis becomes more complicated than simply knowing where an emergency department is located.

The problem becomes whether the patient can remain communicative and present long enough to receive help without reproducing the conditions he fears.

Years later, during an acute suicidal crisis, I encountered that problem directly.

I had active urges.

I had a plan.

I was uncertain I could remain safe.

I contacted 988.

And I continued writing.

That later moment cannot be understood fully without the history that preceded it.

Stanford was the origin of the medical trauma as I experienced it.

Dr. Chiang's instruction to seek care outside Stanford marked the point at which that unresolved history crossed institutions.

UCSF became the receiving system.

And eventually, when ordinary care itself had become difficult to approach, writing became one of the few forms of care I could still enter without first surrendering the means by which I remained present.

That is where **Writing as the Act of Care** begins.
