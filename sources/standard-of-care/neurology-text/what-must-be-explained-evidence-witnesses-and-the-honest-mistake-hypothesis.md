# What Must Be Explained: Evidence, Witnesses, and the Honest-Mistake Hypothesis

Before the essays that follow ask what January 24, 2019 means, I want to state how I believe the underlying record should be examined.

My account should not prevail merely because I am the patient. Dr. Steven Lee McIntire’s account should not prevail merely because he was the neurologist. The relevant question is which explanation best accounts for the available evidence while preserving the distinction between what was directly observed, what was recorded, and what is later inferred from those observations.

That distinction is especially important because the evidence is not uniform. A contemporaneous statement answers a different question from a step count. An eyewitness answers a different question from a longitudinal observer. A medical note establishes what a physician recorded, but not automatically that every recorded maneuver occurred exactly as described. A later clinician can establish what was observable later, but cannot by that observation alone establish what another physician intended earlier.

The record therefore has to be examined by source and by scope before it is assembled into a causal explanation.

## The encounter itself

I was present during the January 24 examination.

So was my wife.

Dr. McIntire was the examining physician.

That gives the encounter three people physically present, but two firsthand accounts independent of the physician’s later written note: mine and my wife’s. Those accounts should not be described as independent in the statistical sense—we are married, shared the event, and could influence one another’s recollection—but each of us was physically present and capable of reporting what we personally saw and heard.

My account includes statements I attribute to Dr. McIntire, including “Good for you,” “Talk about your shoulder,” “This is not a strep throat clinic,” and “You don’t have MS.”

I remember difficulty performing tandem walking without using the wall for support. I remember severe bilateral leg pain arising during the encounter. I remember saying:

**“Now, my legs are dead.”**

I also describe subsequent physical contact as nonconsensual and experienced the encounter as an assault.

Those statements establish what I report experiencing.

They do not, without further analysis, establish the legal classification of the conduct or the neurological mechanism of what followed.

My wife’s testimony occupies a different evidentiary position from that of everyone who knew me outside the room. She was present for the examination and can provide her own account of what she observed there. Where her recollection agrees with mine, that agreement is relevant. Where it differs, the difference is equally relevant.

The point is not that two people automatically defeat a medical record.

The point is that the examination was not witnessed only by the physician who documented it.

## The medical record

Dr. McIntire’s note is contemporaneous medical evidence and should be treated seriously.

It also contains findings I dispute.

The record reportedly describes normal gait and station, normal tandem gait, a negative Romberg, and no dysmetria on finger-to-nose or heel-to-shin testing.

My account differs.

I remember requiring the wall during tandem walking. I also dispute that finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin testing occurred as documented.

Those are factual disputes about the examination itself.

The note proves that those findings were recorded.

My account establishes that the patient who underwent the examination contests them.

My wife’s presence supplies another firsthand source potentially capable of bearing upon some of those disputes.

The proper evidentiary response is therefore not to treat either the chart or my recollection as self-authenticating. It is to identify the conflict and ask what other evidence constrains the possible explanations.

Some questions are relatively simple.

Did I touch or use the wall while attempting tandem gait?

Were particular examination maneuvers performed?

What words were spoken?

Others are substantially harder.

What neurological significance should have been assigned to what was visible?

What did Dr. McIntire actually recognize?

What did he infer?

What did he intend?

Those questions require different evidence. Observable conduct should not be confused with conclusions about motive.

## What was visible before the examination

I did not arrive at Stanford neurologically normal.

There were already manifestations of impairment, particularly involving balance and movement. People without neurological training had observed that something was wrong. I had sought neurological care precisely because symptoms already existed.

That fact has limited but important evidentiary force.

It does not establish what Dr. McIntire actually noticed.

It does not establish that he understood the cause.

It does not establish intent.

It does mean that a theory in which there was simply no observable neurological abnormality available to anyone at the time has to confront evidence that abnormality was visible outside a specialist examination.

The relevant distinction is:

**observable abnormality existed** is an evidentiary proposition.

**McIntire knowingly ignored it** is a causal and mental-state conclusion requiring additional proof.

I make arguments about the latter elsewhere. The methodological point here is to prevent the former from disappearing merely because the latter remains contested.

## The longitudinal witnesses

There is also a much larger body of people who knew me across time: family members, friends, colleagues, teachers, neighbors, and others who encountered me before or after January 2019.

I have described this group broadly as numbering in the hundreds across the periods of my life relevant to establishing how I functioned.

That should not be misunderstood to mean that hundreds of people witnessed the Stanford examination, or that every person observed me both immediately before and immediately after January 24.

They did not.

Their potential evidentiary role is longitudinal.

Different people observed me in different contexts and at different times. Some knew my mobility and activity before January 2019. Some observed my deterioration afterward. Some knew me across both periods.

Their testimony can therefore bear upon function and change.

It cannot independently establish what happened in McIntire’s examination room, and it cannot by itself determine the medical cause of the later decline.

That limitation makes the testimony more useful, not less, because it identifies exactly what question it can help answer:

**Was the person observed after January 24 functioning differently from the person known before it?**

## The quantitative baseline

Apple Health supplies another source that is independent of retrospective description.

During the thirty-one days ending January 24, 2019, my recorded walking averaged approximately **3.06 miles and 7,128 steps per day**, totaling about **94.7 miles**.

Across the broader pre-event baseline, my averages were approximately **2.27 miles and 5,279 steps per day**.

The immediate month therefore represents substantial ambulatory activity and was, by those measures, more active than my broader baseline.

The first month after the event recorded approximately **1.11 miles and 2,699 steps per day**.

By the final month of 2019, the corresponding values were approximately **0.19 miles and 575 steps per day**.

The evidentiary proposition is straightforward:

**recorded ambulatory activity fell markedly after January 24 and continued to deteriorate across 2019.**

The causal proposition is different:

**the January 24 encounter caused or accelerated that deterioration.**

I believe there is a causal relationship.

Apple Health alone does not prove it.

The data provide temporal and functional evidence that any causal explanation must account for. They do not identify the mechanism.

That distinction is one of the governing rules of this series.

## The before-and-after record

Other dated material can contribute to the same reconstruction: contemporaneous writing, clinical records, photographs, video, communications, and other records whose timing can be established.

These sources have different strengths.

A video can preserve visible movement.

A contemporaneous message can establish what I was reporting at that time.

A clinical note can establish what a clinician observed or recorded.

A photograph can establish appearance at a particular moment.

None alone resolves causation.

Their importance lies in whether independent sources converge upon the same chronology.

That chronology is the object requiring explanation.

## The Medical Board record

A portion of the evidence also left my personal possession and entered a regulatory process.

I submitted material to the Medical Board of California. The Board conducted interviews with both me and my wife.

That fact should not be overstated.

It does not mean the Medical Board adopted my allegations, found misconduct, or reached the conclusions I reach.

It means that portions of the evidence, our accounts, and the chronology were supplied to an independent state regulator and are not merely a narrative constructed for these essays years later.

To the extent that the Board retains those materials, they form part of the external evidentiary history of the dispute.

## What clinicians outside Stanford observed

The chronology also includes neurological abnormality recognized outside Stanford.

Soon after entering the UCSF system, abnormalities were observable to clinicians there within an examination substantially shorter than the years over which the dispute has subsequently unfolded.

That later recognition has to be used carefully.

It cannot establish retrospectively what McIntire saw.

It cannot establish his state of mind.

It cannot prove that the same findings existed in precisely the same form on January 24.

But if neurological abnormality was readily apparent soon afterward, and if manifestations had also been visible to people without neurological training beforehand, then an explanation based on complete clinical invisibility becomes less satisfactory.

The distinction is again between observation and inference.

**Later clinicians observed abnormality.**

From that fact one may reasonably ask what was observable earlier.

One may not simply infer intent.

## The evidence hierarchy

The record therefore has an internal hierarchy determined by the question being asked.

For what occurred inside the examination room, the most direct sources are the people who were there and the contemporaneous documentation: McIntire, my wife, me, and the medical record.

For my functional condition before and after the encounter, longitudinal observers and dated records become more important.

For the magnitude of change in ambulatory activity, Apple Health provides quantitative evidence.

For what I reported at particular times, contemporaneous communications and records matter.

For subsequent clinical manifestations, later clinicians can report what they themselves observed.

For intent, professional misconduct, medical causation, or criminal liability, none of those categories alone is sufficient. Those are conclusions that must be drawn from the combined record under the appropriate professional or legal standard.

This prevents one source from being forced to do work it cannot do.

It also prevents uncertainty at one level from erasing evidence at another.

I do not have to prove McIntire’s intent in order to establish that my ambulatory activity changed.

A step count does not have to establish causation in order to establish a functional discontinuity.

My wife does not have to diagnose neurological disease in order to describe what she witnessed.

A later neurologist does not have to know what McIntire thought in order to document what was visible during a later examination.

Keeping those propositions separate allows them eventually to accumulate without becoming confused.

## The honest-mistake hypothesis

This is where the possibility of honest error should be examined.

Physicians make mistakes.

Neurological presentations can be difficult.

Symptoms fluctuate.

Patients compensate.

An office examination samples only a short period of a much longer disease course.

A clinician can misinterpret a finding without malice, just as a patient can misunderstand part of an encounter without dishonesty.

For those reasons, honest mistake is a legitimate hypothesis.

But it is a hypothesis.

It is not an explanation merely because the words have been spoken.

An adequate honest-mistake account would need to explain the record rather than bypass it.

It would need to address what neurological manifestations were available to observation before and during the appointment.

It would need to address why my recollection of tandem walking differs from the documented finding.

It would need to address the disputed coordination testing.

It would need to incorporate my wife’s eyewitness account rather than treating the encounter as though physician and patient were the only people present.

It would need to explain how my immediate reports during the encounter should be understood.

And it would need to propose a clinically plausible account of the substantial functional decline that followed without assuming that temporal association itself proves either my causal theory or its opposite.

The same obligation applies to me.

If I argue that the January 24 encounter caused or accelerated my deterioration, I must account for the natural history of my disease, competing neurological explanations, the limits of the available measurements, and any evidence inconsistent with my account.

The causal standard should therefore be neither:

*it happened afterward, so the examination caused it*,

nor:

*causation has not been adjudicated, so the temporal discontinuity is irrelevant.*

The proper question is comparative:

> **An alternative explanation must account for the whole record with evidentiary specificity and explanatory power at least comparable to the account it seeks to displace.**

That is the standard I ask readers to apply to every account, including mine.

The absence of adjudication leaves some conclusions open.

It does not place the underlying evidence into suspension.

The patient’s report exists.

The wife’s eyewitness testimony exists.

The contemporaneous medical documentation exists.

The disputed examination findings exist as disputes.

The pre-event manifestations require explanation.

The quantitative before-and-after change requires explanation.

The longitudinal observations require explanation.

The material supplied to the Medical Board forms part of the history.

The observations made outside Stanford form part of the chronology.

From this point forward, the later essays need not repeatedly establish those foundations.

They can begin from a simpler methodological rule:

**Preserve the facts each source can support. Keep inference visibly separate from observation. Then compare explanations by how completely and coherently they account for the record.**

That is what must be explained.
