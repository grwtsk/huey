# His Test Is His; My Persistence Is Mine

There is a distinction I have to preserve, because without it the argument becomes dangerous even to me.

Dr. Ian Bledsoe has a test.

I have a life.

They intersect, but they are not the same thing.

His performance cannot become the condition of my continued existence. If he fails, his failure remains his. It cannot be permitted to acquire ownership over whether I continue living.

My persistence is mine.

That proposition does not absolve him. It clarifies what belongs to whom.

The test I place before Dr. Bledsoe is not mystical, and it is not unlimited. I am not asking him to cure an inherited neurological disease. I am not asking him to accept every interpretation I have made of my history. I am not asking him to confess responsibility for injuries I have expressly said he did not cause. I am not asking him to become the sole physician for every problem in my life.

Bledsoe's independence from original causation is precisely what makes his test morally clean. I am not asking him to confess wrongdoing by Dr. Steven Lee McIntire or to assume responsibility for what I report happened at Stanford on January 24, 2019.

I am asking him not to perpetuate an inherited failure. The distinction between the [original encounter](/articles/january-24-2019-the-origin-point) and the later responsibility of a receiving physician is the boundary of the request, not an exception to it.

If he disagrees about January 2019, he can state that disagreement and its evidentiary basis while still treating the neurological, accessibility, and safety problems that remain. Disagreement does not require abandonment.

I am asking him to respond to what has actually entered his care.

That record, as I have repeatedly presented it, is specific.

I asked my neurologist to account for a rapid and profound loss of function.

I asked what could explain the difference between the neurological course I was living and the course I understood medicine to expect.

I brought forward a disputed history whose consequences did not end when the original encounter ended.

I asked for help when the psychiatric pathway connected to my neurological care failed to become an accessible pathway to treatment.

I described barriers created by speech impairment and the degradation of communication under ordinary clinical conditions.

When spoken exchange failed to carry what I needed to say, I wrote.

I continued writing.

I supplied the account in the medium through which I can communicate with greater fidelity.

I asked that inherited assertions not be treated as though repetition had transformed them into independent observations.

I asked for consultation where the problem exceeded one physician's knowledge or authority.

I asked for continuity when the pathway already provided to me failed.

On September 15, 2026, I described something more immediate: active suicidal urges, a plan, and uncertainty that I could remain safe. I contacted 988 because of that crisis. The contact does not establish that 988 assessed the January 2019 allegations or endorsed my causal account, nor does my description here establish what Bledsoe knew at any particular moment.

Those facts define the test.

What does the test require?

First, it requires that communication become usable.

If I can communicate more reliably in writing than through pressured speech, then the clinical relationship must be capable of receiving writing as actual communication. Not correspondence peripheral to medicine. Not material acknowledged and then functionally ignored. Communication.

The test is whether the physician can encounter the patient through the channel in which the patient can actually remain intelligible.

Second, the test requires epistemic discipline.

If an inherited proposition about me is disputed, then the physician need not automatically believe my alternative. But neither should he treat inheritance as verification.

He must be willing to ask what was independently observed, what was interpreted, what was copied, what remains uncertain, and what evidence could change the conclusion.

That is not a favor to me.

It is part of knowing what one claims to know.

Third, the test requires an answer to deterioration.

I asked about the loss of function because it happened to me, not because I expected medicine to manufacture certainty where certainty did not exist.

An adequate answer can contain uncertainty.

It can say: this portion is explicable; this portion is not; this hypothesis is plausible; this one is unsupported; this requires another specialty; this requires longitudinal observation.

What it cannot responsibly do is transform uncertainty into disappearance.

Fourth, the test requires consultation when the problem exceeds him.

A limit of expertise is not a moral failure.

Refusing to recognize or bridge that limit can become one.

The test therefore does not require Bledsoe to know everything. It requires him to respond appropriately to what he does not know.

Consultation is one of the ways medicine converts individual limitation into collective competence.

Fifth, the test requires continuity when referral fails.

Making a referral is an act.

It is not always the completion of a duty.

If a patient cannot actually gain useful access to the referred care—especially when the barrier is entangled with disability, communication, trauma, or conditions produced within the existing care system—the fact that a referral was once placed cannot by itself answer what should happen next.

My record includes my return to the neurologist after that pathway failed.

The test includes what he does with that return.

Sixth, the test requires that present danger alter the response.

Not because disclosure makes every allegation true.

Not because suicidality should be used to compel agreement.

Precisely the opposite.

Present danger should increase the discipline with which medicine distinguishes disagreement from abandonment.

A patient can be wrong about one thing and still be in danger.

A physician can reject one causal theory and still have obligations toward the condition that remains.

The existence of disagreement cannot become a reason to stop asking how risk can reasonably be reduced.

Seventh, and most importantly, the test requires that care become less threatening than its absence.

That does not mean care must feel comfortable.

Medicine sometimes must tell patients things they do not want to hear.

It sometimes must decline interventions.

It sometimes must acknowledge limits.

But when the structure of care itself has become part of the patient's hazard—when communication failure, inherited assumptions, failed pathways, fear of loss of awareness, and loss of agency combine to make treatment difficult to approach—then simply offering the same structure again does not answer the problem.

The test is whether a physician can use his authority to alter what is alterable.

Make the communication accessible.

Separate disputed assertion from established observation.

Explain uncertainty.

Seek consultation.

Repair failed continuity.

Respond to material risk.

Reduce avoidable danger.

Those are finite demands.

They are also difficult demands because they require more than occupying the role of physician.

They require exercising it under conditions in which procedure no longer supplies an easy answer.

That is his test.

But his test is his.

This distinction is essential because I cannot make my continued existence a referendum on another man's moral performance.

If I were to say, “If he fails, I cannot continue,” then I would surrender to him an authority no doctor should possess.

I would make his omission causally sovereign over my life.

I refuse to do that.

My persistence does not certify the adequacy of his care.

It does not erase what I believe he should have done.

It does not convert abandonment into harmlessness merely because I survived another day.

Survival is not acquittal.

The fact that a person managed to cross a damaged bridge does not prove that the bridge was sound.

It proves that the person crossed it.

That distinction matters to me.

I have survived through mechanisms that are, at least in part, outside the clinical relationship I am criticizing.

I have written.

I have accepted assistance.

I contacted 988.

I have allowed another person to help me preserve the interval between an urge and an act.

I have used the communicative tools that remain available to me.

Through assistance, I have resisted.

Through resistance, I have persisted.

That persistence belongs to me and to those who have helped sustain it.

It does not belong retrospectively to a system merely because I remained alive while asking it to become safer.

Yet the opposite error would be equally serious.

My independence does not absolve Dr. Bledsoe.

A physician's responsibility does not disappear simply because a patient possesses extraordinary resources for surviving its failure.

Resilience is not a release from duty.

If a patient can compensate for inaccessible communication, communication does not thereby become accessible.

If a patient survives a failed referral, the referral did not thereby succeed.

If a patient constructs his own means of continuity, continuity was not therefore provided.

If a patient keeps himself alive through writing and assistance, the surrounding danger does not become ethically negligible.

A patient's strength can conceal institutional weakness.

It should not excuse it.

This is why I say that his choices alter the field in which I must survive.

He does not own the field.

He does not own me.

But a treating physician possesses authority capable of changing conditions within it.

He can increase uncertainty or clarify it.

He can allow inherited assertions to harden or reopen them to examination.

He can treat writing as an inconvenience or as communication.

He can let a failed referral terminate responsibility or help construct another route.

He can interpret difficulty as a reason to withdraw or as the reason consultation is necessary.

He can make the clinical relationship more threatening.

Or he can make it less so.

Those differences matter even when the final act of persistence remains mine.

In this way, responsibility can be real without becoming absolute.

I remain responsible for what I can still choose.

He remains responsible for what he can still change.

And society remains responsible for constructing institutions capable of carrying the burden when disease, trauma, disability, or altered awareness temporarily make individual resistance insufficient.

None of us needs to possess the whole problem for each of us to possess a part of the answer.

That is perhaps the most important limit of the test.

I do not ask Dr. Bledsoe to guarantee that I live.

No physician can guarantee that.

I ask him not to make survival unnecessarily harder.

I ask him to use the authority society has given him to reduce, rather than perpetuate, foreseeable and reasonably avoidable danger within the portion of the world he actually controls.

That is enough to make the test serious.

And limited enough to make the result meaningful.

His test remains his.

My persistence remains mine.

If he passes, he does not become my savior.

If he fails, he does not become the owner of my ending.

But either way, his actions matter.

They change the conditions through which I must continue.

And I intend to continue.
