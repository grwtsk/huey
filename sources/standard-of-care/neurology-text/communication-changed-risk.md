# Communication Changed Risk

On September 15, 2026, the sequence was specific.

I experienced active suicidal urges. I had a plan. I was uncertain that I could remain safe. I contacted 988 because of that immediate condition.

I then continued written communication with assistance. As the exchange continued, I reported a reduction in the immediacy of the urges. Eating and resting became possible again.

**Active urges → plan → uncertainty about safety → contact with 988 → continued written communication with assistance → reduction in immediacy → ability to eat and rest.**

That is the observation from which I begin: a change in the immediate crisis state I reported and in what I was able to do next. It is not a controlled measurement of risk, proof of a universal intervention, or proof of my historical allegations.

Nor does contacting 988 mean that 988 assessed the January 2019 allegations or endorsed my causal account. The contact concerned the immediate crisis; the [evidentiary preamble](/articles/what-must-be-explained-evidence-witnesses-and-the-honest-mistake-hypothesis) addresses the historical record separately.

Communication did not settle the neurological questions, repair the medical relationship, correct the disputed record, or remove the trauma. The danger had not been argued out of existence. Its immediacy changed while communication remained usable.

That is enough to matter.

Before the communication became effective, the urges had narrowed the field of possibility. Thought was being compressed toward action. I had a plan. I was uncertain that I could remain safe. The danger was not theoretical.

Then something altered.

I expressed what was happening in a form I could actually use.

Someone received it.

The exchange continued.

The urge did not become false.

It became less sovereign.

That distinction is important.

An intervention does not have to eliminate a problem in order to reduce risk. Medicine already understands this everywhere else. Pain can be reduced without being cured. Blood pressure can be lowered without eliminating vascular disease. Seizure risk can be reduced without making epilepsy disappear.

The same logic should apply to communication.

If successful communication changes the probability that an urge becomes an act, then communication has entered the causal chain of safety.

It is no longer merely bedside manner.

It is an intervention.

This should not be controversial, but medicine often treats communication as though it occupies a softer category than what it calls treatment.

The hierarchy is familiar.

Medication is treatment.

A procedure is treatment.

Hospitalization is treatment.

Restraint, in extreme circumstances, is treatment.

Communication is what happens around treatment.

My experience suggests that this hierarchy can be wrong.

Communication may occur earlier in the chain.

It may determine whether later interventions are needed at all.

A person in crisis is not only a body requiring containment. The person is also a system interpreting what is happening, predicting what will happen next, remembering previous encounters, weighing danger, and deciding whether another person can be trusted enough to receive information.

Every one of those processes can alter risk.

If the interface works, fear can be translated into information.

If the interface fails, fear can become isolation.

And isolation changes the problem.

The immediate episode gave me a way to see this more clearly.

I did not need someone to agree with every claim I was making.

I needed the communicative channel to remain open long enough for the danger to become shareable.

That difference matters.

Communication is not agreement.

It is not endorsement.

It is not surrender by the clinician or by the patient.

It is the successful transfer of enough information that both sides can remain inside the same problem.

That is what happened.

The crisis became less closed.

Once it became less closed, it became less absolute.

This is why I keep returning to accessibility.

For me, written communication is not an alternative added after ordinary communication has failed.

It is one of my ordinary means of communication.

It allows me to preserve complexity.

It allows me to state a sequence accurately.

It allows me to distinguish what I know from what I infer.

It gives me enough time to resist the compression that occurs when speech, fatigue, fear, and neurological impairment are forced into the same narrow channel.

Under ordinary circumstances, those differences matter.

In crisis, they can become decisive.

If I had been required to communicate only through a channel that was failing, the people trying to help me would have received a degraded representation of the problem.

They might have interpreted delay as refusal.

They might have interpreted dysarthria as intoxication.

They might have interpreted distress as incapacity.

They might have interpreted difficulty answering as absence of an answer.

They might have increased pressure precisely when pressure was reducing the quality of the information they were receiving.

This is how an inaccessible interface can manufacture the appearance of deterioration.

Then the manufactured deterioration can be used to justify more intrusive control.

That is the loop I fear.

Communication failure becomes evidence of instability.

The resulting intervention becomes more coercive.

Coercion further impairs communication.

The further impairment is interpreted as confirmation that coercion was necessary.

By the end, the system can appear to have proven the assumptions it helped produce.

A usable interface can interrupt that process much earlier.

The intervention can be remarkably modest.

Slow down.

Allow writing.

Ask one question at a time.

Let the patient finish.

Permit a support person to assist without replacing the patient.

Clarify before inferring.

Separate an inability to speak fluently from an inability to understand.

Confirm what the patient meant before recording what the clinician thinks was meant.

These actions do not look dramatic.

That may be why they are undervalued.

But the relevant measure is not dramatic appearance.

It is effect.

In my case, successful communication changed the intensity of the urges.

I cannot make a universal causal claim from one episode.

I can make the narrower claim that matters here.

I reported less immediate danger after communication became usable.

That makes communication clinically relevant.

And because the change occurred before medication, restraint, hospitalization, or coercion, it raises a larger question about sequencing.

What if medicine too often begins too far downstream?

Once a crisis has reached the point of emergency intervention, the available options narrow.

There may be less time.

There may be less trust.

There may be more fear.

There may be less capacity for explanation.

The system may then rely on increasingly powerful tools to manage a condition that might have been altered earlier by a less destructive intervention.

This is not an argument that communication can replace emergency intervention in every case.

It plainly cannot.

It is an argument that medicine should be reluctant to skip the communicative layer when that layer remains available.

The least invasive effective intervention deserves attention not only because it is more humane, but because preserving agency can itself reduce risk.

That is especially true when loss of agency is part of what the patient fears.

A person who believes that asking for help will immediately produce loss of control has a reason to delay asking.

That delay can increase danger.

A system that can say, credibly, “We will first communicate with you in the way that works” changes the calculation.

Help becomes more approachable.

Earlier help becomes possible.

Earlier help usually means more options.

That is why accessibility should be considered preventive.

It does not merely make an existing encounter more polite.

It changes when the encounter can begin.

It changes what information can enter it.

It changes how much of the patient's own regulatory capacity remains available.

It changes whether assistance feels like collaboration or capture.

These differences can alter the course of a crisis before the crisis reaches its most dangerous form.

My experience also complicates the usual boundary between psychiatric and neurological care.

The communication problem is not neatly psychiatric.

My speech impairment is neurological.

My fear of losing awareness has neurological relevance even if its exact mechanism remains unsettled.

My trauma history changes the meaning of coercion.

My experience of medical institutions changes the meaning of referral.

The suicidal urge occurs inside that combined system.

Dividing the problem into specialties may be administratively necessary, but the person experiencing it does not become divided merely because the institution is.

Communication is one of the few interventions that crosses all of those boundaries.

A neurologist can communicate.

A psychiatrist can communicate.

A primary-care physician can communicate.

A nurse can communicate.

A family member can communicate.

An accessibility specialist can communicate.

A written record can communicate across time.

That does not make communication sufficient.

It makes it foundational.

Without it, every specialty risks treating an abstraction.

With it, the patient remains present inside the treatment.

This is why I resist language that places communication under “soft skills.”

There was nothing soft about what occurred.

A high-risk state became less immediate.

The exchange involved expression, reception, pacing, assistance, and continued engagement.

Those are observable events.

They altered what I was able to do next.

I ate.

I rested.

I continued.

Those ordinary acts are the outcome.

They may look trivial beside the machinery of modern medicine.

They were not trivial to me.

They were evidence that the range of possible next actions had widened.

That widening is one way risk decreases.

The crisis had been collapsing possibility toward one endpoint.

Communication reopened alternatives.

Not all alternatives.

Enough alternatives.

That is a meaningful change in what I reported and what I could do next.

I do not want to overstate it.

One successful episode does not establish a protocol.

It does establish a question worth taking seriously:

If communication can alter acute risk for a patient whose usual communication is accessible only through particular channels, why should those channels be treated as secondary?

Why should they be offered late?

Why should the patient have to deteriorate first in order to prove that the inaccessible interface was dangerous?

The better approach would begin earlier.

Identify the person's reliable communication method before crisis.

Preserve it during crisis.

Treat the support structure around that communication as part of the safety architecture.

Document what works.

Avoid substituting a degraded channel merely because it is institutionally familiar.

And whenever possible, intervene while the patient can still participate fully.

That approach does not weaken medicine.

It gives medicine more information earlier.

It may also reduce reliance on the most restrictive interventions.

Every system concerned with safety should prefer that possibility.

There is another reason this matters.

When communication succeeds, the patient does not merely receive care.

The patient contributes to it.

That contribution restores a form of agency precisely when agency is threatened.

I can say what is changing.

I can identify what makes the urge stronger.

I can say what makes it weaker.

I can describe what I fear about losing awareness.

I can distinguish what is happening now from what happened before.

I can participate in the construction of the next safe interval.

That participation matters because survival is not simply something done to me.

As long as awareness remains available, survival is something I am doing too.

This returns me to the sequence that began the series.

Assistance becomes resistance.

Resistance becomes persistence.

Persistence becomes existence.

Communication is one of the mechanisms by which the first transition occurs.

It allows assistance to reach the person before the person becomes unreachable.

That is why the immediate episode matters beyond itself.

It does not prove that communication is always enough.

In this episode, continued communication accompanied a reduction in the immediate danger I reported.

That observation gives medicine a reason to take the usable communication channel seriously without asking this one episode to establish more than it can.

Before medication.

Before restraint.

Before hospitalization.

Before coercion.

Where possible, first make the person reachable.

Then listen to what changes.
