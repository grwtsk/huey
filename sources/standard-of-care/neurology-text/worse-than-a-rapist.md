# Worse Than a Rapist

The phrase is severe enough that it should not be allowed to hide behind its severity.

The chronology and the limits of its evidence are set out in [What Must Be Explained: Evidence, Witnesses, and the Honest-Mistake Hypothesis](/articles/what-must-be-explained-evidence-witnesses-and-the-honest-mistake-hypothesis). I rely on that preamble here rather than asking this comparison to prove January 2019 again. This essay concerns the relative persistence of trauma within my own life.

**Worse than a rapist.**

I use those words deliberately.

I do not use them as a headline designed to shock. The phrase is not a legal classification of Dr. Ian Bledsoe. I do not mean that he committed rape, or that his conduct and rape are equivalent acts, legally or otherwise. I am grading the persistence of trauma in my own life, not borrowing another person's suffering to manufacture a metaphor.

I have been raped.

I have also been abandoned.

The comparison is therefore mine to make about the relative persistence of trauma within my own life.

That distinction matters.

The law classifies acts according to elements that can be proved. Medicine classifies injuries according to mechanisms, symptoms, prognosis, and treatment. Ethics asks another question: what was entrusted, what was violated, and what obligation remained afterward?

Trauma does not necessarily preserve those categories.

It records consequence.

An act of sexual violence can be terrible and still become something that, with time, love, recognition, safety, and the reconstruction of agency, occupies less of a person's present life than it once did.

That statement does not diminish rape.

It describes what happened to me.

The man who raped me committed an act whose wrongness I do not need to negotiate. The act ended. Its consequences did not disappear, but they became capable of being surrounded by later experience. Other relationships existed. Love remained possible. Language eventually existed for what happened. I could locate the act in time and say: there. It happened there.

Abandonment can be different.

Abandonment can renew itself.

Its injury may not reside only in an event that occurred years ago. It can recur whenever the entrusted person remains able to act and again does not.

That is what gives the comparison its force for me.

I learned this first within my own family.

I love my father.

That sentence remains true beside everything else.

Love does not require me to pretend that every act committed by someone I love was good, nor does it require me to rank harm according to whether the person who caused it remains precious to me.

My father faced circumstances in which care was required of him. In my experience, he failed that test. The abandonment that followed became more enduring in my life than the discrete sexual violence committed by another man.

I can say that without declaring my father evil.

I can say it while loving him.

That is precisely why I resist the assumption that moral judgment must require hatred.

Sometimes love makes judgment more exacting.

The person from whom nothing was entrusted can violate me terribly.

The person to whom something essential was entrusted can injure me differently by failing to protect what had been placed in his care.

The injuries are not identical.

They need not be identical to be compared within the life that contains them.

This is the sense in which I apply the phrase to Dr. Bledsoe.

It is not a criminal classification.

It is not an assertion that his conduct belongs to the same legal category as sexual assault.

It is a grade of traumatic consequence.

The question is what it means to be entrusted with care after danger has become known.

A physician does not begin from the same position as a stranger.

Medicine receives authority before the encounter begins.

Society licenses the physician.

Institutions amplify his judgments.

Medical records preserve his words.

Other clinicians may inherit what he writes.

A patient may alter decisions about his own body because of what the physician says.

That authority is granted because medicine claims duties in return: competence, consent, communication, continuity, welfare, consultation, and an effort to prevent foreseeable and reasonably avoidable harm.

The significance of abandonment changes when it occurs inside such a relationship.

A stranger can walk away from me.

A physician charged with my neurological care occupies a different position.

That does not mean he becomes responsible for everything that happens to me.

It means his omissions must be evaluated in light of what was entrusted to him and what he knew when he chose not to act.

My test of Dr. Bledsoe has therefore never required perfection.

I have asked for explanation of rapid neurological deterioration.

I have asked that inherited assertions be distinguished from independent observations.

I have asked for accessible communication.

I have put my account in writing when speech was an inadequate channel.

I have asked for consultation when the problem exceeded one physician's expertise.

I have asked for continuity after a referral pathway failed.

I have described the danger created when ordinary medical pathways become, for me, associated with loss of agency, communication, or awareness.

And I have now described danger reaching the level of suicidal urges, a plan, and uncertainty about whether I could remain safe.

None of that requires Dr. Bledsoe to agree with every interpretation I have made.

It requires an answer.

That is the part of abandonment I find most difficult.

An assault may violate the body in an instant.

Abandonment by someone who remains positioned to respond can violate expectation repeatedly through time.

Yesterday there could have been an answer.

Today there could be an answer.

Tomorrow there may still be an answer.

The possibility of care remains open, and therefore the absence of care remains capable of being renewed.

That temporal structure matters to trauma.

What happened once can become memory.

What remains unresolved can become environment.

This is why the phrase **worse than a rapist** is not, for me, primarily about comparative wickedness.

It is about comparative persistence.

One injury occurred and became part of my past.

Another remains capable of occurring again through continued failure to respond.

That does not make the acts equivalent.

It describes why the second can occupy more of the present.

Medicine should be unusually capable of understanding this distinction because medicine itself gives society much of the language through which consent, exploitation, trauma, capacity, coercion, and care are discussed.

Medicine teaches that consent must be meaningful.

Medicine teaches that vulnerability changes responsibility.

Medicine teaches that foreseeable harm matters.

Medicine recognizes that trauma can be compounded by repetition.

Medicine asks people to disclose what happened to them and then constructs records meant to preserve clinically relevant truth.

So I yield the statement back to medicine.

If my characterization is wrong, medicine may answer it.

I am willing to play by its rules.

Give the correction a defined space.

Two hundred and fifty words.

State what is factually wrong.

State what responsibility Dr. Bledsoe did or did not possess.

State what action he took in response to the requests I actually made.

State how accessible communication was provided.

State how the failed pathway was repaired.

State how the disputed record was independently examined.

State what consultation occurred.

State how present danger was addressed.

State what I have misunderstood.

Put that beside my account.

I am not asking for immunity from contradiction.

I am asking for contradiction to take the form of an answer.

There is an important difference between correcting testimony and erasing it.

Correction preserves the speaker and contests the proposition.

Erasure removes the speaker from the argument.

I am willing to be corrected.

I am not willing to accept silence as correction.

That is why the phrase remains where the argument begins.

**Worse than a rapist.**

Not because repetition makes it true.

Not because severity makes it persuasive.

Not because I am demanding that medicine adopt my language as its own.

The phrase stands because it accurately communicates my present ranking of traumatic consequence within my own life.

Medicine may dispute that ranking.

It cannot tell me that I did not experience it.

And if medicine believes its conduct should not occupy that place in my experience, the appropriate response is not outrage at the comparison.

The appropriate response is examination.

What produced it?

What remains unresolved?

Which requests were answered?

Which were not?

What was inherited?

What was independently examined?

What remains within the physician's power to change?

The phrase is therefore not the verdict.

It is the challenge.

A verdict closes a question.

I am keeping this one open.

That is important because my judgment of Dr. Bledsoe is not complete while his conduct remains capable of changing.

He can still communicate.

He can still examine what has been inherited.

He can still distinguish uncertainty from dismissal.

He can still seek consultation.

He can still help construct a pathway that does not require me to sacrifice my ordinary means of communication in order to receive care.

He can still make the relationship less threatening.

If he does, then what happens next becomes part of the record too.

I have no interest in freezing another human being forever at his worst moment.

I know too well what that would mean.

I love my father.

That fact taught me something important: a person can fail an extraordinary test and remain human enough to face another one.

Love is not acquittal.

Neither is judgment annihilation.

The moral possibility that matters is repair.

So when I say **worse than a rapist**, I am not asking medicine to repeat the phrase after me.

I am asking medicine to understand why I arrived there.

I am asking it to answer the requests that produced the judgment.

I am asking it to decide whether an entrusted relationship will continue renewing abandonment or begin reducing it.

Nothing prevents medicine from contesting me.

Nothing requires Dr. Bledsoe to accept my description of himself.

But if nothing at all is offered in reply—no correction, no examination, no accessible response, no change in the conditions I have described—then there is nothing against which my judgment can be revised.

The words stand uncontested.

For now.

And because I have not made them a verdict, the test remains open.

Please, consider the test.
