# The Unordinary Test of Goodness

I can begin with a concession.

Dr. Ian Bledsoe may satisfy many ordinary measures of medical competence.

He may be knowledgeable. He may be respected by colleagues. He may understand the literature of movement disorders and cerebellar disease. He may perform the ordinary functions of neurological practice competently. He may meet many of the standards by which a physician is evaluated in the abstract.

That is not the claim I am making against him.

I am not trying to prove that he is globally incompetent.

The problem is harder than that.

He has inherited an unordinary test of goodness.

By inherited, I mean something precise: a patient directed into the UCSF system after the failure of care I describe at Stanford; a disputed January 24, 2019 encounter; substantial functional deterioration; my wife as an eyewitness; longitudinal evidence; impaired speech; trauma associated with medical care; and unresolved neurological questions. The [prologue](/articles/before-the-examination-the-patient-who-arrived-at-stanford) establishes that history and distinguishes its sources. Bledsoe did not cause the original event I report, was not present at its beginning, and did not author every condition that followed.

But inheritance changes when knowledge is acquired.

A physician can inherit a patient, an injury, a disputed record, a history of failed care, an unresolved danger, and the consequences of decisions made before the physician arrived. None of this makes him responsible for the past merely because it reaches him.

It does, however, place something in his hands.

That something is the next decision.

**Will he independently evaluate what he inherited and use his authority to make continued care safer?**

The concrete acts are written-first communication; investigation of the deterioration; review that distinguishes the provenance of inherited assertions from independent observations; consultation where his expertise reaches its limit; continuity after a failed referral; and an accessible route back into care.

Those requests do not require omnipotence, confession, or agreement with every conclusion I have reached. They require the exercise of the authority already entrusted to him.

Goodness here is not personality.

It is not kindness of manner.

It is not whether a physician is pleasant during an appointment.

It is not even identical to technical skill.

Goodness appears when skill, authority, uncertainty, and vulnerability meet under pressure.

A person can be technically excellent and still fail that test.

Ordinary competence becomes consequential here because the pathway has failed.

That is why I resist making this argument about whether Bledsoe is, in the abstract, a good or bad man.

I do not know enough about his whole life to make that judgment.

I do not need to.

The relevant object is narrower.

I can ask whether he is acting well toward the person whose neurological care he has accepted.

That is already enough.

Professional authority is not morally empty.

A neurologist is given access to the body, the record, the diagnostic process, the language of disease, and the institutional pathways through which other clinicians will understand the patient.

That authority has consequences.

A sentence in a note can outlive the examination that produced it.

A judgment can travel through a record.

A refusal to revisit an inherited premise can make that premise harder to challenge.

A failure to communicate can be mistaken for a failure of the patient.

A failure to act can leave the existing danger exactly where it was.

This is why omission is not always neutral.

Sometimes doing nothing means that the inherited arrangement continues.

And when the inherited arrangement is itself part of the danger, continuation becomes a choice.

That does not mean every omission is wrongful.

It means omissions must be examined in context.

If a physician knows that communication is failing, that the patient experiences the care pathway as threatening, that prior assumptions are disputed, that the patient is describing serious risk, and that the ordinary route has become unstable, then the question is no longer merely whether the physician caused the problem.

The question is whether he is now helping maintain it.

That is a different form of responsibility.

It is prospective rather than retrospective.

It does not ask him to confess guilt for the past.

It asks him to decide what he will do with the present.

I think this is where medicine's moral language becomes most useful.

Medicine already understands that treatment is not merely the application of technique.

It is a fiduciary relationship.

The physician is entrusted with unusual authority because the patient is often vulnerable precisely where the physician is strong.

The physician knows the system.

The physician speaks its language.

The physician can order consultations.

The physician can alter the record.

The physician can document disagreement.

The physician can change how the next clinician encounters the patient.

The physician can say that a communication accommodation is medically important rather than optional.

The physician can distinguish uncertainty from certainty.

The physician can reduce the amount of force required for care to occur.

These are forms of power.

The ethical question is how that power is used.

For me, this is where goodness becomes measurable.

Not perfectly measurable.

Not reducible to a score.

But observable in conduct.

Curiosity under difficulty, attention to a disputed record, usable communication, and willingness to consult are observable in conduct. They give goodness a meaning beyond reputation.

They are the conditions under which character enters practice.

There is another reason this matters to me.

I do not need Bledsoe to become my adversary in order to hold him accountable.

In fact, the argument is stronger if I refuse that simplification.

I can believe that he possesses real medical competence.

I can believe that he entered medicine for reasons he considers honorable.

I can believe that he may have helped many patients.

None of these possibilities resolve the test before him.

A person can have done good elsewhere and still fail here.

A physician can be competent in ordinary conditions and inadequate in extraordinary ones.

The unordinary test does not erase the ordinary good.

It asks whether the ordinary good survives moral difficulty.

That is why this test is not punitive.

It is diagnostic.

It reveals what professional identity is made of when its assumptions are challenged.

If the physician's goodness depends upon the patient fitting comfortably inside the existing system, then the goodness is fragile.

If it survives the difficult patient, the disputed history, the inaccessible communication channel, the institutional discomfort, and the need to question inherited authority, then it becomes something more serious.

It becomes trustworthy.

Trust cannot be commanded.

It is generated by conduct.

In my case, trust would begin with the concrete acts already identified. Their value would lie in whether they changed the conditions of care, not in the grandeur of the gesture.

Not perfection.

Not confession.

Not agreement with everything I believe.

Action.

The physician does not need to become a hero.

He needs to become less dangerous than the arrangement he has inherited.

That is the standard I am trying to articulate.

It is demanding because authority should be demanding.

The greater the power entrusted to a profession, the less satisfying it is to say that the machinery prevented the right thing from being done.

The machinery is part of what professional authority is supposed to navigate.

This is especially true when the patient cannot navigate it alone.

That is where medicine proves what it means by care.

The answer will be visible in what he independently examines and what he changes.

That answer will not measure his whole life. It will show what his professional commitments become when abstraction ends.

And that is where I believe medicine should be judged.

Not where the pathway is easy.

Where the person is difficult to protect.

Where the record is disputed.

Where the institution is uncomfortable.

Where communication must be rebuilt.

Where inherited authority must be examined rather than obeyed.

Where the physician has to decide whether his role is merely to occupy a position within medicine or to use that position for the welfare of the human being in front of him.

That is the unordinary test of goodness.
