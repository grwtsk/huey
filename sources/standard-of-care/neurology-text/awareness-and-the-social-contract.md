# Awareness and the Social Contract

The hardest question in what I have been describing begins at the point where persistence may no longer be mine to exercise.

So far, I have written about resistance as though the resisting self remains continuously available: I experience danger, I recognize it, I communicate it, another person responds, and together we preserve enough time for the danger to recede.

That sequence depends upon something prior to all of it.

Awareness.

I must remain sufficiently present to recognize what is happening, to understand that an urge is an urge rather than an instruction, to remember what I have decided, to use the tools available to me, and to ask another person to help me continue.

When that faculty is available, autonomy has somewhere to stand.

But what happens if it is not?

The concern has a history. My family experience of altered awareness is part of the background against which I recognize the possibility of losing capacities I presently rely upon. That history gives me reason for concern; it does not prove what my own neurological course must be or turn inheritance into destiny.

My own reported episodes of lost or altered awareness are a separate part of the record. So are my experiences with medication and the questions they raise for me about awareness and agency. Those sources of concern should not be collapsed into a single diagnosis or used to settle a mechanism I have not established.

The chronology also includes earlier trauma, the reactivation I associate with [January 24, 2019](/articles/january-24-2019-the-origin-point), the Stanford aftermath, and the unresolved history that crossed into UCSF. That history helps explain why the prospect of losing agency during care carries particular fear for me. It does not establish that every episode has the same cause.

Together, these concerns make it important to preserve intentions expressed while my awareness and communication are strongest. Family history, my own reported episodes, medication experiences, and trauma matter here as distinct reasons to prepare, not as interchangeable proof of an inevitable future.

I do not need to settle the ultimate neurological mechanism here. The practical problem comes first. If the faculty presently allowing me to resist self-harm becomes impaired, then telling me simply to exercise better judgment no longer answers the problem.

Judgment itself is one of the things at risk.

That is where individual autonomy reaches one of its natural limits.

Autonomy is often discussed as though it means leaving a person alone to make decisions. That is only one form of it. Autonomy also depends upon conditions that permit a person to remain capable of making and communicating decisions in the first place.

Language must work.

Memory must hold long enough.

The person's expressed intentions must remain available.

Other people must know how to interpret the means by which those intentions are communicated.

And when one of those functions becomes temporarily unavailable, someone or something must preserve continuity until the person can again participate fully.

This is where I encounter the social contract.

I do not mean an abstract political theory written above human life.

I mean the practical promise by which I live among other people: that when a function necessary to protect myself becomes temporarily unavailable, I will not be treated as though I have ceased to exist.

Something will carry what I temporarily cannot.

A family member may carry memory.

A written statement may carry intention.

An accommodation may carry communication.

A clinician may carry specialized judgment.

An institution may carry continuity.

Law may carry boundaries around what may and may not be done.

No single one of these becomes the person.

Their purpose is the opposite.

They preserve enough of the person's agency that it can be returned to him when he is again able to exercise it.

This is why medicine matters so much in my argument.

Medicine is one implementation of that social contract.

Society gives physicians unusual knowledge, unusual access to the body, unusual authority, and unusual credibility. In exchange, medicine accepts obligations that become especially important when illness makes a person less able to protect himself through ordinary means.

The arrangement is not supposed to be ownership.

It is stewardship.

And stewardship becomes meaningful precisely where capacity becomes fragile.

If I remain fully articulate, fully mobile, fully able to advocate for myself, and fully capable of leaving any encounter that becomes unsafe, relatively little is demanded from this structure.

The difficult case begins when those assumptions fail.

If my speech becomes unreliable, medicine must still be able to hear me.

If physical disability prevents an ordinary form of participation, medicine must still be able to include me.

If my ability to communicate deteriorates under stress, the structure should not respond by increasing the very pressure that makes communication deteriorate further.

And if awareness itself becomes unstable, medicine must have some means of preserving the intentions I expressed while awareness was available.

Otherwise the social contract disappears at precisely the moment it is supposed to become useful.

That is why accessibility is not adjacent to this problem.

Accessibility is part of whether the contract exists at all.

A right that cannot be exercised through the communication channels actually available to a disabled person is not fully usable as a right.

Consent depends upon communication.

Refusal depends upon communication.

Correction depends upon communication.

Reporting pain depends upon communication.

Describing fear depends upon communication.

Explaining what has happened before depends upon communication.

Even the statement “I am losing the ability to resist this” depends upon communication reaching another person accurately enough to matter.

If the interface fails there, the failure is not merely technical.

The social promise has failed to cross the interface.

This is especially important for me because my ordinary communication does not begin and end with speech.

I write.

I construct.

I make tools.

I use visual forms.

I use music.

These are not decorative extensions of a more authentic spoken self.

They are among the ways the self becomes legible.

When I write before awareness becomes less reliable, I am not merely creating a record of what I once thought.

I may be constructing continuity for a future version of myself who temporarily cannot reconstruct it alone.

That gives writing another function.

It can become a bridge across states of awareness.

I can say while I am present: this is what I fear; this is what helps; this is what I do not want confused for consent; this is how I ordinarily communicate; this person may assist me; this account should remain available if my speech or behavior later becomes difficult to interpret.

Such writing does not eliminate the need for judgment.

It gives judgment a more faithful object.

It allows the person who exists across time to remain represented when the person visible in a single moment is impaired.

This is not an argument for unlimited medical authority.

The danger of paternalism becomes greater, not smaller, when awareness is uncertain.

A system can easily claim that it is protecting a patient while silencing the patient's previously expressed wishes, excluding the very communication tools that preserve those wishes, or treating temporary impairment as permission to erase the person's broader history.

That would turn the social contract inside out.

The point of carrying a function temporarily unavailable to me is to preserve my agency, not replace it permanently.

A good system therefore asks not merely, “What can we do now?”

It asks, “What did this person tell us when communication was strongest?”

“What accommodations preserve the greatest amount of participation now?”

“What intervention is necessary, and what intervention merely becomes easier once resistance has been weakened?”

“How do we return control as soon as control can safely be returned?”

Those questions matter because awareness does not have to be perfectly continuous for personhood to remain continuous.

The person before an episode and the person after it are not strangers merely because the interval between them was impaired.

The social contract exists partly to carry that continuity through the interval.

This is where the failures I have described become more than failures of convenience.

If I have repeatedly told physicians that I fear states in which awareness or self-control becomes unreliable, and if those same physicians know that speech is an imperfect channel for me, then the design of care must take seriously the possibility that my best opportunity to protect future autonomy is to communicate while I can do so reliably.

That means the writing matters before the crisis.

The support person matters before the crisis.

The accessibility plan matters before the crisis.

The distinction between observation and inherited assertion matters before the crisis.

Continuity matters before the crisis.

The time to construct the bridge is not after the person has already fallen into the gap.

This is why I resist the idea that autonomy and protection must always be opposites.

They can become opposites when protection is coercive, careless, or indifferent to the person being protected.

But protection can also be the means by which autonomy survives a period during which autonomy cannot fully protect itself.

That is the social contract I am asking medicine to honor.

Not: take control because I am vulnerable.

Rather: use your position so that vulnerability does not erase the person who entered your care.

Preserve communication.

Preserve continuity.

Preserve previously expressed intention.

Reduce unnecessary threat.

Use the least destructive means compatible with genuine safety.

And return authority to the person as fully and as quickly as the person's condition permits.

That is what medicine's social authority should purchase.

It should make temporary incapacity less dangerous.

It should not make the prospect of incapacity itself something I must fear because I do not know whether the system will still recognize me when I can no longer argue for myself.

This returns me to the immediate problem.

I have been able to resist because I have been able to communicate.

Assistance has reduced the intensity of urges that had become dangerous.

I am grateful for that.

But I do not want to test the hypothesis that awareness will always remain available just because it is available now.

A safety system built upon the assumption that I will always be able to rescue myself from a state I have warned may impair my ability to rescue myself is not really a safety system.

It is reliance on luck.

The better arrangement begins earlier.

While I can write, I tell you what the danger is.

While I can reason, I tell you what preserves my reasoning.

While I can choose, I tell you what helps preserve the ability to choose.

While I can communicate, I construct the interface through which I hope I will remain recognizable if communication becomes harder.

That is not surrender.

It is an exercise of autonomy extended through time.

And that is why I understand the social contract not as the moment when society takes over, but as the structure by which society helps prevent a temporary loss of capacity from becoming a permanent loss of the person.

Medicine is one custodian of that structure.

Accessibility determines whether I can reach it.

And when awareness itself is the faculty at risk, the quality of that interface may determine whether the person who asked for help remains present long enough to receive it.
