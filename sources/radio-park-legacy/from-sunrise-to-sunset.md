The car gave out just outside of Missoula, Montana. It wasn’t anything dramatic—a sputter, a lurch, and then
that sinking feeling in my gut as I pulled off to the side of the road. The engine gave up like an old man sighing
after a long day’s work. There was a station down the road, they told me, so I left the car there and walked to a
diner to pass the time while they figured out what was wrong.
Missoula. I’d never even thought about stopping here, but the town immediately felt different. The early
morning air was cool, crisp in a way that Georgia never is. Back home, the humidity sticks to your skin like a
heavy blanket, even in the early hours. But here, it was fresh, almost freeing. The sun was barely rising, casting
long shadows across the streets as I made my way to the diner. The few people out that early seemed relaxed, in
no particular rush, like the day stretched out endlessly before them.
I slipped into a booth by the window at the diner, the kind with cracked vinyl seats and coffee that’s strong but
a little burnt, just the way I like it. There was something about the place that felt familiar, even though it was
nothing like home. The waitress—she looked about 25 but carried herself with the weariness of someone much
older—brought me coffee and a smile. Her name tag read "Lily."
As I sat there, the sun rising higher, I couldn’t help but compare this place to home. In my town, a small
conservative dot on the map of Georgia, there’s a constant sense of urgency. People rush, always with a purpose,
heads down, shoulders hunched as if the weight of the world rests solely on their backs. You can feel it in the air
—an invisible pressure pushing everyone forward, faster and faster, even though no one’s entirely sure where
they’re going.
Here, it was different. The whole town seemed to breathe easier. People moved slower, not in laziness, but as if
they had time to spare. I wondered if that’s what it was—if Missoula gave its people permission to slow down,
to be present, in a way that my town never did.
Around mid-morning, a group of young people shuffled in, all dreadlocks, tattoos, and hemp clothing. They sat
in the corner, laughing softly amongst themselves. Back home, they would’ve been stared at, maybe even
whispered about. But here, they were just a part of the scenery, like they belonged. It felt like this town had
room for everyone, a concept that was foreign where I’m from. We like things neat, organized, and familiar—
any deviation is met with suspicion.
“Need anything else, hon?” Lily asked, topping off my coffee for what felt like the fifth time.
I shook my head, glancing at the clock. Noon already. The mechanic hadn’t called yet. “How long you lived
here?” I asked, curious about the people who called this place home.
“All my life,” she said, leaning against the booth. “People come and go, but I can’t seem to leave. It’s too...calming,
I guess.”
“Calming?” I echoed, trying to understand what she meant.

She nodded, her eyes softening. “Yeah. Like, people here don’t get caught up in all the noise. Everyone’s just
trying to live their lives, you know? You don’t have to fit into some mold. You can just...be.”
That struck me. Back home, there’s a mold for everything. How to act, how to talk, how to live. Deviating from it
gets you noticed, and not in a good way. In Missoula, the mold seemed to melt away.
I spent most of the afternoon watching people come and go. An older couple sat down at the counter, both
wearing matching hiking boots and fleece jackets. They talked about their morning hike up the Bitterroot
Range like it was the most natural thing in the world. In Georgia, the only time you’d hear people talking about
hiking would be if they were taking a weekend trip to the Smoky Mountains, and even then, it wouldn’t carry
the same nonchalance. Here, it seemed like being outdoors was just part of life, part of the rhythm of the town.
As the afternoon wore on, a middle-aged man with graying hair and glasses sat down across from me. His plaid
shirt looked worn, like it had seen more than its fair share of winters. He ordered coffee and a sandwich,
nodding at me with a friendly smile.
“You’re not from around here, are you?” he asked, his voice gentle, not probing.
“No, just passing through,” I said, stirring my coffee.
“Where from?”
“Georgia,” I answered.
He raised an eyebrow. “That’s quite a ways. What brings you out here?”
“Family,” I said, leaving it at that.
He nodded, taking a sip of his coffee. “Missoula’s a good place to stop. It’s a strange town in some ways, I’ll
admit, but there’s a sense of community here that you don’t find everywhere.”
“Strange how?” I asked, curious.
He chuckled. “Well, we’re kind of a bubble, you know? You wouldn’t expect a place like this in Montana, but
here we are. People look out for each other. There’s room for different perspectives, different lifestyles.”
I thought about that as the sun began its slow descent. A bubble. That was the word. Missoula felt like its own
little world, separate from the rest of the country. It wasn’t just about the politics, though that played a part. It
was the way people moved, the way they interacted with each other, with the town itself. There was no rush, no
invisible pressure to conform. People here lived their lives on their own terms, and it was jarring for someone
like me, used to the rigidity of small-town Georgia.
As sunset painted the sky in soft pinks and oranges, the diner started to empty out. The mechanic finally called,

telling me the car was ready. I stayed a little longer, watching the light fade through the window, reluctant to

Lily came by one last time, wiping down the booth next to me. “Heading out soon?” she asked.
“Yeah, just waiting for the check.”
She smiled, and there was something so open, so genuine about it, that it caught me off guard. “Safe travels,
hon. Hope you come through again.”
I nodded, feeling a strange pang of loss. In another life, maybe I could’ve stayed in a place like this. A place
where you could breathe, where life wasn’t measured in how well you fit into a mold but in how you lived each
moment. But I wasn’t from here, and I knew I had to get back on the road. My daughter needed me, and my life,
for better or worse, was waiting for me back in a world that didn’t know how to slow down.
As I left the diner and walked toward the station, the air had cooled, and the town felt quieter. I realized that
what I’d felt all day—the ease, the openness—was something I hadn’t experienced in a long time. Maybe ever.
Missoula, this strange little bubble, had shown me what life could look like if we weren’t all rushing toward
something undefined, something we weren’t sure we even wanted.
I climbed back into my car, the engine humming steadily now, and as I pulled away, I couldn’t shake the
thought: how many of us are trapped in a life that isn’t really ours? How many people, like me, are left behind by
a world that tells us we have to keep up, fit in, conform? And how many more lives will be destroyed if we don’t
find a way to rise together, to slow down, and make room for everyone, like this town did?
Missoula had shown me that it was possible, that maybe, just maybe, we didn’t have to do it alone.
Dear Reader,
I’ve spent my life walking down difficult roads, and now my daughter stands at the edge of a darkness that I fear she 
won’t come back from. This story isn’t just mine—it’s one that reflects the struggles of many. We’re all trying to find 
our way in a world that feels like it’s unraveling. I’m asking you to share this journey, not to focus on the hardship, 
but to remind others that there’s strength in coming together. Let’s lift each other up before more are left behind in 
the shadows.
Warmly,
Margaret Anne Thompson
