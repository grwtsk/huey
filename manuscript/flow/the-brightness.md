### The Brightness

#### The First Day

It was early spring, sometime around April or May, after the melt had gone down into the ground and the world had started showing itself again.

The nettles were already out.

New nettles.

Soft-looking things with their little green violence waiting in them.

I stood outside my mother’s bedroom door and listened.

Usually I could tell she was there without seeing her. A board would complain beneath a foot. Cloth would move. A drawer would open. Something would be set down, taken up, dropped, folded, dragged. A cough. A chair. The thousand little proofs by which one human being tells another, without intending to:

*I am still here.*

That morning there were none.

No floorboard.

No cloth.

No drawer.

No breath I could catch through the door.

There was quiet, hush, vacancy.

There was the kind of silence that has weight.

I listened harder.

Nothing.

And somehow, before anybody told me anything, before I had evidence enough for an adult word, the child in me already knew the adult word.

*Suicide.*

We lived in a house between two soft mountains, up high where the valley between them had nearly become a summit itself. From the road the house did not tell the truth about its height. It began low, maybe one regular story. But behind it the earth fell away sharply, and the back of the house kept going down with it.

My mother’s room was back there.

Maybe three stories above the hill.

A wooden deck ran along the back.

I ran downstairs.

Ran, tore, bounded, dropped through the house.

Under my mother’s room was the unfinished basement. My things lay everywhere on the floor. Pink fiberglass sat between the studs beneath clear plastic. The walls were walls only by intention.

My grandmother’s upright piano stood there.

I sat down.

There was middle C.

I struck the black keys **like the lightning sting of a red-pepper cat.**

I struck the white keys **like thunder kicking the bottom out of a washtub.**

Harder.

Again.

The clear plastic over the pink insulation trembled **like skin stretched over a fever.**

The piano did not stop when my hands stopped.

The strings went on.

The boards went on.

The harmonics found the bare joists and unfinished corners and made the whole underside of the house answer me.

Then something answered from above.

Panic.

Running.

Stomping.

Her floor was my ceiling.

There was no finished ceiling there, no insulation to swallow anything, so her feet passed over my head **like boots running across the lid of a box I had been shut inside.**

Fast.

Then faster.

Then the deck door flew open.

Bang.

I ran to the sliding glass door beneath the deck.

I looked up.

My mother was there.

Above me.

Standing on the rail.

That is one picture.

Then she was not there.

That is the next.

Below me, down the hill, among the new nettles, lay my mother.

Her body had come out of the sky **like a coat dropped empty from a high hook.**

She lay thick among the green.

Still.

Stopped.

Unmoving.

I watched.

I waited for some correction to what I had seen.

There was none.

She did not move.

This is the first day.

I need to say that plainly because two other days are going to enter it.

They did not happen while my mother lay in those nettles.

They happened at other times.

Another year.

Then years afterward.

But memory does not always keep time the way a calendar keeps time. There are moments that open wider than the minutes assigned to them. There are seconds with rooms in them. There are silences big enough to store whole years.

My mother lay motionless below me.

My own body stood motionless above her.

And the space between one heartbeat and the next became long enough to contain two other days.

---

#### The Second Day

The second day happened separately.

Later that year, perhaps.

Or perhaps late in the year before.

I cannot make the date more exact without pretending.

I will not pretend.

A man had begun coming into our house.

I will not name him here.

Here I will name only those who can live with their names.

He was dating my sister.

She was barely sixteen.

He had a close-shaven face that hid some of his age, long hair, a hollow look about him. If you looked quickly, perhaps you could take him for a high-school senior. He seemed to understand the usefulness of being mistaken for younger.

He smiled for my parents.

Smiled and charmed and pleased.

He buttered them.

His smile went over them **like butter melting across hot bread.**

Then he would look at me.

The smile would still be there.

The look beneath it was something else.

It came through **like a nail showing from the far side of a board.**

He brought disorder into the house by degrees.

Chaos can arrive politely.

That is something I learned.

Drugs went into the food.

Drops of acid went into the juice.

A little.

Then a little more.

Then again.

Weeks.

Enough repetition and the extraordinary acquires furniture. It sits down at the table. It knows where the glasses are kept.

He stayed too much.

One day I came home.

My parents were gone.

I went downstairs.

He was on top of my sister.

She was motionless beneath him.

I knew enough to know what I was seeing.

I did not know enough to know what to do with having seen it.

I ran.

There was a closet.

I got inside.

The closet took me **like a mouse hole taking the last small thing alive when the boots enter the kitchen.**

I hid.

I stayed.

Minutes went somewhere.

Then hours.

I do not know how many.

Eventually the body makes demands even when terror has made the rest of you very small.

I needed the bathroom.

Bathrooms already meant something in our house.

My mother was sick.

SCA14 was part of it.

It was not all of it.

There was something else in her suffering, something I could observe but could not then name.

She would barricade me in the bathroom.

Hours.

Hours and hours.

She made me clean the mirror.

Again.

Again.

Again.

She did not like what she saw.

So I cleaned what she saw.

Wipe it.

Look.

Again.

Wipe it.

Look.

Again.

The bathroom was perhaps the size of a clinician’s examination room.

Tile.

Sink.

Mirror.

Door.

Wall.

Inspection.

Correction.

Repetition.

After enough hours, enough demands, enough years, the room became almost clinical to me.

A place where somebody else looked and I was required to correct what they saw.

That day I opened the door.

He was there.

The look.

The glare.

Pot covered the counter.

Everywhere.

His eyes were red.

He grabbed my arm.

He pulled me into the room.

He pushed me against the wall where the towel rack was mounted.

The hooks pressed into me **like little iron teeth testing where they might hold.**

And then I was gone.

That needs its own sentence.

I was gone.

Not gone from the bathroom.

Not necessarily unconscious in the ordinary meaning of that word.

Gone from command.

Gone from choice.

Gone from the bridge between knowing something is happening and making the body answer.

I had eyes somewhere in the world.

I had a body somewhere in the world.

But I no longer had reliable possession of the road between them.

There is what happened to my body.

There is what I remember.

Those are not the same record.

I know where my memory ends.

I know where it begins again.

Between those two points I will not manufacture a bridge.

The next thing I possess is the bathroom.

The drain was open.

Water was running.

It went away **like the house had decided there was nothing there worth keeping.**

I was on the hard floor.

My body hurt.

There was pain inside my anus.

And there was a hole in my chest.

Not a hole anybody could bandage.

Not flesh torn open where another person could see it.

A vacancy.

A rupture.

An opening so absolute that the nearest language I have, even now, is that God had opened my chest.

Then came the brightness.

It entered from above.

At an angle.

I know that angle.

It is the angle at which I look toward Christ in an icon now.

But the icon came later.

The brightness came first.

Radiance.

Whiteness.

Fire without burning.

Light without lamp.

Something larger than the image through which I would one day recognize it.

A billion billion billion white suns in one blink.

No sound.

I did not hear God.

I will not add a voice because there was no voice.

I saw.

I felt.

The brightness entered the opening in my chest.

Not around me.

Into me.

Heart.

Breath.

Spirit.

Something restarted.

Something returned.

Something said life without using words.

Afterward my sister went into foster care.

I became homeless.

That is another sentence adults can surround with paperwork.

Placement.

Custody.

Housing.

Jurisdiction.

Responsibility.

Disposition.

A child has another vocabulary.

Gone.

Left.

Cast off.

Forsaken.

Abandoned.

I was abandoned after what happened to my sister.

I was abandoned after what happened to me.

I use that word deliberately.

Abandoned.

Morally.

Familially.

And, as I later came to understand the obligations adults had toward children, legally too.

But at the time I still did not know what the adults had known.

I did not know what decision had been made after the knowledge reached them.

That knowledge belonged to the third day.

---

#### The Third Day

The third day came years later.

I was grown.

At least my body was.

There are questions that stay children longer than the people carrying them.

A question can spend twenty years waiting at the same door.

I asked my mother about the rape.

Not generally.

Not about trauma.

Not about what a doctor might call my response to it.

I wanted to know what happened after.

What did you know?

When did you know?

What did you do?

What did Dad do?

What happened when the adults finally possessed the knowledge the children had been carrying?

She told me my father had wanted to call the police.

She said she stopped him.

Held him back.

*Call the police?*

That was my first thought.

Not relief.

Not gratitude.

*Call the police?*

By then those words already had another history inside me.

I knew what it meant to seek authority and not necessarily receive protection from it.

I knew—or believed I knew—that a telephone call did not turn automatically into safety, truth, investigation, consequence, repair.

The police might not have worked.

That was the hidden sentence beneath my question.

But they had not even reached that failure.

Something simpler stopped them.

My mother explained.

The man was already going to prison in California.

An unrelated charge.

He was due there within days.

So, she had reasoned, why make matters more complicated?

Complicated.

Complex.

Messy.

More trouble.

Another proceeding.

Another authority.

Another story somebody would have to tell.

The word sat between us **like a stone somebody had decided was light enough to leave on a child’s chest.**

*Complicated.*

A child had been raped.

My sister had been raped.

My father wanted to call the police.

My mother held him back.

Not because nothing had happened.

Not because she had no reason to believe it.

Not because the man was beyond reach.

Because he was already going to prison for something else.

Because another action would make things complicated.

My father had represented the Air Force to the Army.

That was part of who he had chosen to be.

Structure.

Duty.

Service.

Chain of command.

Responsibility.

And his children had been raped.

Still, according to what my mother told me, complexity was enough.

I had already lived through abandonment once.

Homelessness had taught me the physical form.

No house.

No ordinary protection.

No reliable accompaniment.

But on the third day I learned another form.

Abandonment can happen while everyone remains standing in the room.

Nobody needs to walk away.

Nobody needs to close a door.

Sometimes the abandonment is the decision not to cross the distance that duty requires.

Parent.

Guardian.

Protector.

Physician.

Friend.

Husband.

Wife.

Anyone who, by love, office, parenthood, profession, promise, oath, or simple human choice, has taken the place beside another person.

Accompaniment is not proximity.

A person can stand close enough to touch you and still leave you alone.

That knowledge reached me on the third day.

The rape had taught me what one person could do to the body.

The later knowledge taught me what other people could decline to do after they knew.

Those were different injuries.

The first came through violation.

The second came through knowledge followed by omission.

And the second entered a chamber the first injury could not reach, because that chamber had been reserved for the people who were supposed to come afterward.

The ones who were supposed to say:

I see you.

I believe enough to act.

You do not carry this alone.

I will accompany you into whatever comes next.

Instead:

*Complicated.*

That is what I mean when I say that abandonment can be worse than rape.

I mean *to me*.

I am not measuring another survivor’s suffering.

I am not making rape small enough to be surpassed casually.

I know its magnitude because I know it from inside.

What made abandonment reach deeper was precisely that it came afterward.

The stranger, the predator, the violator had already shown me what he was capable of taking.

The people bound to me by their own choices were where I still kept the expectation that something might be given back.

Protection.

Witness.

Action.

Accompaniment.

When that expectation failed, the injury did not repeat itself.

It descended.

There was one more thing the third day eventually acquired.

My sister.

Years later she told me what she had seen.

She said she had seen him playing with my butt.

That is her memory.

Not mine.

I do not remember it.

I will not borrow her eyes and call them my own.

I will not grow a memory because somebody who loves me supplies a fact.

There is what she saw.

There is what I remember.

There is what my body later told me through pain.

And there is the black interval where agency and recollection disappear.

Those records touch.

They do not become one record merely because they agree.

I know what I know.

She knows what she saw.

Where I was gone, I say:

I was gone.

That is the whole truth I can give from inside that part.

#### The First Day Again

And all this time—

in the first day—

my mother had not moved.

That must be remembered.

We have not changed dates without saying so.

The second day came at another time.

The third day came years afterward.

But I can place them inside that first stillness now because that is how long the stillness became.

The child beneath the deck had no clock for it.

A motionless mother can stop time.

A child can look at a body and live a lifetime before the body takes its next breath.

So there she still was.

In the nettles.

The new nettles.

Spring's first green teeth.

My mother below.

Me above.

Glass between us.

Silence between us.

And inside that silence:

the man,

my sister,

the closet,

the bathroom,

the mirror,

the hooks,

the drain,

the floor,

the pain,

the brightness,

the homelessness,

the foster home,

the unanswered years,

my father wanting to call,

my mother stopping him,

*complicated,*

my sister remembering what I could not,

and the adult I became trying to testify for the child who had been present but could not remain present in his own body.

All that time could live there.

All those years could pass before one nettle moved.

I watched my mother.

Still.

I waited.

Nothing.

Then—

*wake.*

A breath.

The smallest movement.

Then another.

My mother moved.

She climbed out of the nettles.

Slowly.

I watched every place the plants had touched her.

The stings.

The scratches.

The red marks.

I counted them because children count what they cannot explain.

One.

Another.

Another.

She rose out of that new spring growth **like somebody the earth had tried to keep and could not.**

She came from the nettles.

From the thaw.

From the fresh.

From the green.

Broken.

Bleeding in little places.

Alive.

And the child behind the sliding glass door might have believed that this was the brightest thing he would ever see:

a mother falling three stories,

lying still,

and then breathing again.

A resurrection small enough to happen behind a house.

But I had another day inside me.

Another floor.

Another opening.

Another return.

And I had seen a brightness for which I still have no adequate unit.

So no.

My mother rising from the nettles was a miracle.

But that is not the brightest thing that day.
