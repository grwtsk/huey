# On Ezekiel

## A Reflection of Grace in Radio Park

His name was Eddy.

Some people called him Perro.

I call the moment Ezekiel.

I want to make that distinction before I do anything else, because there are ways of naming a person that begin as description and end as possession. I do not want to take Eddy away from himself and replace him with somebody more useful to my faith. I do not want to baptize his suffering into a symbol because symbols are easier to carry than people.

He was Eddy before I saw him that afternoon.

He remained Eddy after Sister Jenny lifted her hand from his arm.

Perro belonged to another geometry of his life.

Ezekiel belongs to mine.

It is the name I give the place in my memory where a man who seemed almost consumed by what had happened to him remained, somehow, a man.

That distinction matters to me now because there are so many names available when we would rather finish seeing somebody.

Gang member.

Prisoner.

Addict.

Homeless.

Dangerous.

Withdrawing.

Perro.

Each may describe something.

None is large enough to hold a human being.

I knew Eddy as a gang member. I knew prison had passed through his life because prison seemed still to be written across his body. Tattoos climbed his arms and disappeared beneath his clothes. They were not the decorative accumulation of someone casually collecting pictures. They looked acquired through time. Some were dark and crude, some faded, some layered against other marks.

Whatever their individual histories were, I did not know them well enough to tell them.

That is important too.

I saw the ink.

I did not know every story inside it.

His skin itself seemed to be coming apart.

It was burned in places and peeling badly, lifting in thin pieces only to expose more damaged skin beneath. There was something terrible about the repetition. The eye expected the injured layer to come away and reveal healthy skin underneath.

Instead there seemed to be another wound.

Then another.

The body had no obligation to become metaphor for me. Eddy's skin was his skin, and whatever had injured it deserved a material explanation before it deserved a poetic one.

But memory makes associations whether we ask it to or not.

Watching him, I could not avoid thinking about the way suffering sometimes behaves.

We imagine that if we could remove the visible injury, we would arrive at the untouched person beneath it.

Then another layer comes away.

There is grief beneath anger.

Fear beneath violence.

Need beneath defiance.

Memory beneath need.

And beneath all of these there is not necessarily some pristine human being waiting to be recovered, some original version preserved before the first wound.

There is simply the human being.

Still there.

Eddy was withdrawing that day.

I did not need a diagnostic instrument to know his body was having difficulty inhabiting itself. He paced. He shook. His movements carried that awful union of exhaustion and agitation in which the body seems to demand that something happen while providing no safe answer for what that something ought to be.

People gave him room.

Some of that room may have been wisdom.

Some of it was fear.

I was nearby, watching him while carrying things of my own.

I did not know, until that afternoon, how much I was carrying.

Burden can become strange that way. The body learns a weight gradually enough that the weight becomes posture.

Eventually you stop calling it heavy.

You simply bend.

Sister Jenny did not seem to begin with any of the classifications available to the rest of us.

She began with Eddy.

She walked toward him.

That was all.

No committee.

No theory.

No determination that he had become safe enough to deserve kindness.

No demand that he explain himself before she approached.

Whatever she believed had reached her feet before it reached an argument.

She crossed the distance.

I watched her small body come near his damaged one.

The difference in their forms made the scene almost difficult to look at. Eddy seemed assembled out of accumulated warnings. Jenny seemed to carry none.

But I do not want to call her fearless.

That would make the moment too easy.

Fear may have been there.

It simply did not get to decide the distance.

She came close enough to touch him.

Then she did.

Her hand rested on his arm.

On the tattoos.

On the damaged skin.

On the place where prison, injury, withdrawal, and whatever had preceded all three seemed to have written themselves together.

I remember the touch more clearly than I remember any words.

Perhaps because the touch said something language often complicates.

*You are still here.*

Not:

*You are innocent.*

Not:

*Nothing happened.*

Not:

*Everything you have done is forgiven by everyone.*

Not even:

*You will be all right.*

Only:

*You are still here.*

And:

*I am willing to be here with you.*

That is a different kind of statement.

It makes no claim over the past.

It makes a claim upon the present.

My faith has made it difficult for me to treat flesh as spiritually incidental.

Its central movement goes the other way.

Toward a body.

Jenny did too.

Not *the needy*.

Eddy.

Not *the addicted*.

This arm.

This damaged skin.

These tattoos.

This trembling body.

This particular man occupying this particular patch of earth.

Whatever I understand of Incarnation has left me suspicious of any love for humanity that cannot survive contact with a human being.

Jenny's touch did something else I learned to recognize only later.

It did not take possession of him.

She did not make his life evidence of her goodness.

She did not hold him there.

She did not demand that the man receiving the blessing perform gratitude for the woman offering it.

Her hand rested.

Eddy could remain.

Eddy could leave.

He did not pull away.

There was agency even there.

If I were forced now to draw the architecture of those few seconds, perhaps I would still give it four chambers.

Safety.

Relation.

Agency.

Actualization.

Not safety in the childish sense that nothing dangerous existed.

Plenty existed.

Safety in the smaller, harder sense that Jenny did not add another injury.

Relation because she crossed the distance between them without pretending the distance had never existed.

Agency because the touch remained an offering rather than becoming a seizure.

And actualization because nothing in the encounter required Eddy to become somebody else before he could occupy the next moment of his own life.

He could simply remain alive inside it.

Jenny began to pray.

I wish I could give you every word.

I cannot.

Memory does not permit me to place quotation marks around language I no longer possess.

What I remember is her posture.

What I remember is Eddy's.

What I remember is that his body, which had seemed unable to become still, became a little quieter.

Perhaps only for a moment.

I will not turn that moment into a medical miracle.

Withdrawal did not cease because I witnessed a prayer.

Burned skin did not become healthy before my eyes.

The tattoos did not vanish.

Nothing I saw permits me to tell you that addiction ended there, or that a life altered its course because I happened to be watching.

Grace does not require us to falsify the wound.

Something smaller happened.

And perhaps something larger.

A woman touched a suffering man without recoiling from him.

A suffering man allowed himself to be touched.

And I witnessed it.

That was enough to disturb something inside me.

I had thought I was watching Jenny raise Eddy.

Then my soul raised me.

I mean this as exactly as I know how.

Something in me stood before I understood that I had been sitting beneath a weight.

I had not known how heavy my heart had become.

I had been carrying the suffering around me as though carrying it were simply another requirement of being awake. Need everywhere. People everywhere whose stories had become administrative categories in the mouths of those with somewhere else to sleep. Hunger. Cars becoming bedrooms. Bodies negotiating another night. Addiction. Fear. Small acts of generosity disappearing almost as quickly as they were performed.

One can become accustomed to grief without becoming unharmed by it.

Apparently I had.

Then Jenny put her hand on Eddy.

Something in me rose.

My soul got there first.

My body followed.

For a moment I understood that being moved is not always an emotion.

Sometimes it is locomotion.

The heart alters the position of the body.

I rose because she had crossed a distance I had been watching.

I rose because Eddy had permitted that distance to close.

I rose because there, between a small woman's hand and the scarred arm of a man called Perro, I saw something all my abstractions about mercy had failed to contain.

God was not reflected in either of them as possession.

Jenny did not contain God more completely because she was helping.

Eddy was not farther from God because he needed help.

God appeared to me in the relation.

In the refusal to discard.

In the preservation of difference without abandonment.

In the touch that did not require ownership.

In the strange fact that two people could remain entirely themselves while something passed between them that neither could have produced alone.

That is why I call the moment Ezekiel.

Not because Eddy became the prophet.

Not because I have the right to place another man's life inside my allegory.

I had known since childhood of a valley in which everything visible seemed already to have given its answer.

The bones looked like bones.

The question was whether appearance had been granted the final word.

Eddy was not bones.

He was not dead.

He was standing directly in front of us.

But I had already begun, without realizing it, to participate in the smaller death by which a human being becomes indistinguishable from the evidence of his damage.

Jenny corrected me without accusing me.

She touched him.

There was the man.

His name was Eddy.

People called him Perro.

The names did not divide him into different people.

Ezekiel was only the name of what happened inside me when I understood that.

There was another thought I carried away from the park.

It is more dangerous because it approaches medicine, and therefore I want to give it exactly the status it had.

It was a thought.

Not evidence.

Not diagnosis.

Not laboratory result.

Not biological claim.

I had been struck by hepatitis.

Or, more accurately, by what I thought I was not seeing.

Eddy had lived through circumstances that, in my mind, carried risks I associated with hepatitis. Yet I did not see the outward signs I expected to see.

But skin is not a laboratory.

Eyes are not a blood test.

Hepatitis can exist without the visible signs a layperson expects, and I did not possess Eddy's medical record or the authority to infer one from his appearance.

I knew none of that from looking.

What I possessed was wonder.

The same damaged skin that made suffering so visible also made me wonder what remained invisible.

What did this body know that I did not?

What had it survived?

What had failed to enter it?

What had entered and been cleared?

What accidents, protections, vulnerabilities, adaptations, and inheritances were hidden beneath the surface I could see?

For most of the afternoon, everyone could see reasons to avoid Eddy.

Suppose there were also reasons to study him.

Suppose the person society had learned to read primarily as danger also carried information of extraordinary value.

The thought changed the geometry for me.

Then I noticed something dangerous inside the thought itself.

I had almost found another reason he should matter.

Another credential.

Another usefulness.

Another way for a system to permit itself to look at him.

Jenny had needed none of these.

She had touched Eddy before anyone knew what could be learned from him.

His worth did not depend upon becoming medically useful.

If he carried nothing scientifically unusual whatsoever, Jenny's hand would have belonged there just as completely.

A human being does not have to contain a discovery in order to justify being touched with tenderness.

That had to come first.

Only after that could the other question remain honest.

Because perhaps one of our errors is that we search for answers primarily among the bodies we have already decided are respectable enough to teach us.

Medicine examines populations.

Institutions select records.

Researchers define cohorts.

Systems decide what becomes signal and what remains noise.

Nature does not consult those systems before placing an anomaly inside a prisoner, an addict, a homeless man, a saint, a child, a physician, or somebody pacing through a park.

Knowledge may reside wherever a human body resides.

And so the question followed me.

Not as doctrine.

Not as evidence.

As wonder.

Could Eddy—Perro, this suffering man whom I call Ezekiel only for the purpose of remembering what I learned—have carried some clue no one had thought to notice?

Could there have been something within him that mattered to hepatitis?

Could Eddy have carried something medicine had not yet learned to ask for?

I do not know.

That is where the question must remain.

And perhaps the question beneath it matters more.

How many answers have we walked past because suffering made the person carrying them difficult to look at?

How much knowledge has disappeared because we mistook damaged skin for the boundary of a human life?

How many times have we examined the wound so completely that we failed to examine the person?

Sister Jenny solved none of those questions.

She did something antecedent to solving them.

She touched him.

Before diagnosis.

Before usefulness.

Before redemption.

Before proof.

There are systems that require enormous machinery before they can decide what a person means.

There are moments in which a human hand knows the first obligation immediately.

That afternoon in the park, Jenny placed hers upon Eddy's arm.

His skin was still burned.

It still peeled.

The prison ink remained beneath it.

Withdrawal still inhabited his body.

Nothing had been erased.

That is important.

Grace had not rewritten the record.

It had entered the record.

What was wrong remained wrong.

What had happened remained happened.

The good entered beside it and refused to surrender the whole field.

And when I remember the scene now, I do not first see the tattoos.

I do not first see the peeling skin.

I see the small distance between two bodies disappearing.

Then I feel the weight in my own heart that I had not known was there.

Then my soul rises.

And somehow,

my body goes with it.
