### The Words I Was Asked to Remember

There are words I have been asked to remember.

They have stayed with me in ways the tests were never designed to measure.

The first time was at UCSF, in the emergency room. A resident neurologist came to see me almost immediately. There were seconds between my arrival and a neurologist standing in front of me. Seconds, not months. He welcomed me there. He looked at me and seemed to recognize the disease almost at once.

Then he gave me three words.

I could not produce them for him when he asked for them back.

But I remember them now.

**Bus.**

Not an abstract bus. The 147 in Chicago, several of them arriving almost together after the long wait, clumping down the street as though one bus had gathered the others behind it.

**Tree.**

A particular tree. The tree behind the apple, behind the great **A** on the banner in Ms. Yetzel’s first-grade classroom. The alphabet hanging there, and somewhere behind one letter a whole small landscape surviving.

And:

**Purple.**

Purple like the rain.

The words did not disappear.

They went somewhere.

Later, at my first appointment with Dr. Bledsoe, the test came again. Dr. Butts, the neurology resident working with him, gave it to me while Dr. Bledsoe watched. I have met Dr. Butts only that once.

This time there were five words.

I remember those too.

A **castle** I could walk through.

A **face** I could see.

A **daisy** I could smell.

A **vase** with weight in my hands.

And **velvet** beneath my fingers.

Perhaps that is the difficulty.

A word does not always remain a word for me.

It acquires a room.

A surface.

A temperature.

A route through it.

When someone says *castle*, I may not hold five letters followed by two more. I may have corridors. Stone. A way in. A way out.

When someone says *daisy*, perhaps the flower must first exist somewhere before I can return its name.

This can make memory look strangely absent from the outside.

Inside, it may be crowded.

In another room, in another kind of examination, a psychologist gave me a memory test.

There was something I needed her to understand.

My boyhood dog, Sampson, was black.

I told her ten times.

But I did not tell her directly.

I could not.

Instead I placed it into the room around us.

Into action.

Into association.

Into the little movements and references by which thought sometimes has to travel when the direct road from thought to speech will not open.

It was something like trying to communicate from inside an expressionist painting. The object is there. The fact is there. The color is unmistakable from where I stand. But the person outside the frame may be waiting for a sentence.

*Sampson was black.*

I counted.

One.

Again.

Again.

Ten times.

Then I waited until our follow-up appointment to learn whether she had heard me.

There is something very quiet about waiting to discover whether another person heard a scream.

Because that is what it can feel like.

That is what it is.

I am screaming.

No one can hear.

Not because there is nothing being said.

Because what leaves me does not always resemble what another person has been taught to recognize as speech.

And yet memory continues.

Sometimes almost playfully.

I sing to my children.

There is an old marching cadence, the kind used to keep many feet moving together, and I borrow its gait for pi:

*three-point-one-four-one-five-nine,*  
*that’s the number on my mind,*  
*two-six-five-three-five-eight-nine,*  
*six-two-six-two-three-three-six.*

That is usually where the children begin correcting me.

Then they keep going.

Forty digits.

Eighty.

More.

The number passes from my voice into theirs and becomes larger than the little song I made to carry it.

I like that.

Memory does not always have to prove itself by returning three words at the appointed second.

Sometimes it comes home years later.

Sometimes a **bus** is still traveling through Chicago.

Sometimes a **tree** is still standing behind an **apple**, behind an **A**, in a first-grade classroom.

Sometimes **purple** remains rain.

Sometimes a **castle** can still be entered, a **face** seen, a **daisy** smelled, a **vase** lifted, **velvet** touched.

Sometimes a black dog has to walk around the room ten times before anybody notices him.

And sometimes the children take the number from you and carry it farther.
