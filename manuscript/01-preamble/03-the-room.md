## The Room

*Stanford Health Care. January 2019.*

My wife was beside me when we entered.

I had come with neurological symptoms and a family history of ataxia. My balance was already uncertain. My perception of sound had changed. These difficulties had brought me to a neurologist; they had not yet taken away the life I had learned to make around them. There had been work, journeys, ordinary days with my wife and children. I needed help understanding what was happening within that life.

Dr. Steven Lee McIntire was seated in a slumped position. His eyes appeared red. Those details remain in my memory, but I do not know what caused them. They do not tell me what kind of day he had had, or what he understood about the man arriving before him.

I began trying to explain.

When I told him that my perception of sound had changed, he replied:

“Good for you.”

These are the words I remember and have preserved in my letters. The remembered exchanges are not a transcript of everything said in the room.

I had offered the change in sound as something I wanted him to consider. What came back to me seemed to close where I had hoped a question would begin. I kept trying to bring the neurological history into the conversation.

“Talk about your shoulder.”

He returned to that instruction as I returned to the symptoms I had come to describe. I cannot give his reason for the redirection. I can remember the effort of trying again: there was more to say, and I had not found a way to get it before him.

My mother had ataxia. Her history was part of what I had come to explain, part of why I wanted to be understood. The words could be brief. What they asked someone to consider was not.

* * *

He struck my knee reflexes so rapidly that the first leg had not stopped moving before he struck the second. He rolled backward past my wife and said angrily:

“This is not a strep throat clinic.”

I was trying to speak about neurological symptoms.

Then he instructed me to walk heel-to-toe. I attempted it. I could not do it independently.

I used the wall.

It is an ordinary detail to carry for so many years. In the room, the wall was support. I was attempting the walk he had asked for, and I needed it. The distinction between doing the task and doing it unaided had not arrived as an argument. It was in the way I moved.

My wife saw the attempt. She had come with me to an appointment, not to acquire a second occupation as its witness. Later, I would need to say that she was there, then distinguish what she had seen from what I had told her. At that moment, we had not yet entered the work of preserving the encounter.

The distances I had walked elsewhere did not make heel-to-toe walking easy here. Nor did needing the wall cancel the journeys. This was the same body: capable of some things, having difficulty with others, brought into the room because I needed those differences understood.

He screamed:

“You don’t have MS.”

I answered:

“My mother has ataxia.”

He shrugged.

I remember the gesture. I do not know what he understood when I spoke. My answer had returned to the family history, to the person whose illness I knew and whose relation to my own symptoms I wanted examined. I had not come to have every concern confirmed. I had come to find out what could be made of it.

There were only a few words in my reply. Behind them was a life with my mother, and before them was the uncertainty that had brought me there. Neither fitted easily into the exchange.

* * *

During the escalation, crushing pain began in both legs.

I cannot place its beginning upon one reflex strike, one step, or the shrug. Those moments belong to the encounter I remember. I have not identified one of them as the bodily mechanism of what followed. What I can report is that the pain began while I was there, and that I needed time before I could put the change into words.

“Now, my legs are dead.”

The sentence came after the experience had begun. It was not the result of a test or a diagnosis I had made. It was what I could say of what was happening to me.

I have held on to *now*. It was the word that placed the change beside us, in the time of the appointment. The sentence did not explain the pain. It tried to bring the physician to it.

Later, I would describe these words in letters. Those letters have their own dates; they preserve my recollection of speaking, not a recording made that day. I remember the sentence as an attempt to report something new before it had become a history I needed someone to reconstruct.

The balance difficulty was already part of my life. The crushing pain was new. I needed both to remain visible. An account of why I had come could not, simply by existing first, explain everything that happened after I arrived.

The difficulty was no longer only in finding words for pain. I was conscious of what was happening, but I could not quickly turn that awareness into speech or make it interrupt the encounter. The room continued while I was still trying to find a deliberate way to act within it.

I had known that separation during earlier trauma. An event could reach me before my response could reach anyone else. I remained there through the interval. Knowing what was happening did not, by itself, give me the means to stop it.

I was one person, caught in that difficulty. It was not a second, untouched self looking out through a body that had merely failed to obey. Under the overwhelm, automatic behavior could come before I recovered deliberate control. What might be visible from outside and what I could choose were not the same account.

Physical examination continued.

I did not consent to the continued contact at issue.

This is the sequence I have reported: increasing hostility, impaired deliberate control, then unwanted physical contact before I had recovered enough expressive agency to interrupt effectively. I am describing this encounter, not making a rule that distress removes consent from every examination.

I wanted care. That desire had brought me into the room. It remained possible to want help and not consent to what was continuing. I experienced my difficulty as an inability to interrupt, not as agreement.

The knee examination and the requested walk have specific places in my account. The continued unwanted contact does not yet have a complete maneuver-by-maneuver sequence. That part remains to be established. What was done, what permission existed at each point, what he could perceive, and what he believed he was authorized to do all matter to the inquiry. They cannot be recovered by making my account more vivid than my memory permits.

I have called what happened an attack. I experienced the contact as an assault. I retain those words because a gentler description would not be more faithful to what I am reporting. They are my allegations, not notice of a conviction already obtained. The legal questions remain for the authorities competent to test the evidence.

I am not alleging that the Stanford contact was sexual assault or sexual penetration. The earlier rape belongs to another time and another event. What reached between them, in my account, was the experience of authority continuing over my body while I could not make my response effective.

The resemblance has consequences within me. It does not give me access to another person's intentions.

* * *

Before Stanford, the earlier violence had begun to find a place in the past.

It had not become harmless. There had been years of living beside it: relationships, family, love, the ordinary work of continuing. I could remember without every room becoming the room in which I had been injured. I could love my father while retaining the questions about how he had failed me. Love had changed my relation to what remained; it had not altered what happened.

That distance was part of the life I brought to the appointment too. It does not appear in a count of how far I could walk. Yet it mattered that I could enter another room expecting something other than the old injury.

January disrupted it.

I recognized vulnerability before authority, the difficulty of making myself understood, the separation between knowing that something was happening and being able to intervene. These were familiar relations in an unfamiliar setting. I do not place the whole earlier history inside the appointment, or give the physician knowledge of everything it held. I describe what the encounter reached in me.

The past had been something I could approach from a distance. Here, part of that distance gave way.

Later experiences would have their own forms, and some memories would return with a vividness I could not keep at arm's length. They need their own occasions and their own words. I do not bring their sensations backward into this room. What I am describing here is the disturbance of a separation I had lived long enough to begin to trust.

The change in my legs and the change in what medicine meant to me would both have consequences. They are not one explanation. The pain requires its clinical inquiry. The fear and the difficulty of returning to care belong to the history too. Neither should be made to explain the other merely because both appear in my account.

I had gone to a place where examination was meant to help make something clearer. Afterward, the prospect of being examined could also carry the memory of being unable to stop an encounter. To describe that change is not to say that every later clinician repeated what I report here. It is to say that I did not enter the later encounters without it.

* * *

The wall returns when I reach the pages.

In my letters, I describe the resulting Stanford note as recording normal gait and normal tandem walking, along with other normal findings. I dispute that account of the attempt I remember. I used the wall. Leaving out the support changes what the description says I could do.

I also state that finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin examinations were not performed as the note represents. That is a different disagreement. One concerns the description of an action I attempted; the other concerns whether named examinations occurred. Their differences must survive the more general statement that I dispute the visit.

There is now a further difficulty at the edge of that comparison.

January 24, 2019 is the date I have given for this encounter in my letters. A later, assistant-prepared review of preserved clinical-record copies identifies January 22 on the examination note proposed for comparison. That review is a guide to the retained material, not the original note reproduced here. The relationship between the documents bearing those dates remains unresolved.

Two days matter in a chronology. I cannot change my reported date silently, or treat the other date as a typographical error because it would make the account easier to arrange. Nor can I establish that two descriptions concern the same appointment merely by placing them together. The original entry, the appointment history, and any attributable correction must clarify that connection.

The same review identifies a letter bearing May 28, 2020 in which I disputed gait and coordination findings and described needing wall support. That puts another dated account before the inquiry. It is neither a contemporaneous transcript nor an independent witness, and it does not by itself resolve the date conflict. It belongs among the sources through which my account can be followed, rather than leaving the reader to suppose that the September 2026 letters were its first written expression.

The uncertainty concerns a relationship between records. It does not require me to abandon the scene I remember. It requires me to be exact about which source is being asked to answer which question.

A clinical note deserves to be examined in its own terms. So does a patient's objection. I did not watch the choice of every sentence in the note; I cannot provide a scene of its author deciding what to omit. The process of making it remains distinct from the examination I describe. Whether an error occurred, what could explain it, and whether another source changes the comparison are questions the record must remain able to receive.

The wall is still an ordinary wall. What has grown around it is the work of keeping its part in the walk visible.

* * *

My wife was there before there were pages to compare.

Her account cannot be completed by repeating mine, or made independent merely by placing her name behind my words. She can say what she saw and heard, what she remembers without my prompting, and what later discussion may have added. I can preserve that she was present without claiming possession of everything her presence may contribute.

I need the same distinction for the rest of what followed. Later activity records may help describe changes in function; they do not reach backward into the room and reveal an intention. Another examination may illuminate a condition without settling what happened here. An explanation that changes part of my account must be allowed to change it. A correction does not require the whole life to become false.

There are unanswered questions in the encounter, and there are questions about the account I have made of it. I want both examined. What did I manage to communicate? What did he understand? What contact occurred, under what circumstances? What happened in my body, and how should the new pain have been assessed? What does each surviving record actually establish?

Those questions have become difficult to carry. Before they became questions for institutions, however, they belonged to the time I spent trying to reach the person in front of me.

I had come to ask what was happening to me.

While I was still in the room, there was more to ask.
