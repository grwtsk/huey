# Preamble

## The Feather

*Wiesbaden, West Germany. Around 1983.*

I stand on the verdurous crest of a slight rise; nearby a young tree gently ravages in the breeze. I am a child just old enough to play.

The rise is hardly a hill. It is no larger, in my memory, than the root of that young tree lifting the earth around itself, a small green insistence beneath my shoes. There are branches above me below a pale blue sky with its ponderance of dispersion: white.

At my feet is a feather.

It is very small. I look down at it and then up into the tree, because a feather implies a bird. That seems obvious to me. Finding the feather has given me somewhere else to look.

I search the branches for its owner. There are too many, so I count them.

One. Two. Three.

Another forks from the third, and another from that. I follow one outward and lose the place where I meant to return. What began as a branch has become several; the spaces between them begin to compete for the numbers. Trees do not respect the categories of small children. I have found a way to keep looking, though not a way to finish.

I cannot find the bird.

Below me, the feather moves. Not far. The breeze catches an edge and turns it, giving the white underside to the light. For a moment it is difficult to distinguish from the remaining snow. Then I see it again.

My attention moves between the feather and the branches. One is here. The other thing I am looking for is not where I have looked. I can keep going back and forth without having to decide what kind of bird it was or how far away it might have gone.

There is enough of the world to occupy me before I know how it fits together.

My father is a liaison officer, representing the United States Air Force to the Army. Around our life are uniforms and offices, aircraft, fences, insignia: America carried into another country. The adults have work to do with boundaries. East and West. Ours and theirs. Safe and dangerous. Those words will acquire histories for me. For now, much of that world lies beyond the part of the yard that holds my attention.

I look for a balloon.

Somewhere in my imagination is *The Red Balloon*: a boy in Paris and a red companion that seems to have chosen him. I remember its gentleness more readily than I can separate everything I understood then from what I have understood since. Something could come near a child and keep him company. The world could hurt it. Cruelty did not make it the only thing worth watching.

Good is what protects the balloon. Evil is what hurts it. That is the small, fierce arrangement I remember. I do not yet have room in it for all the ways people can cause harm while believing themselves kind.

The sky above Wiesbaden looks large enough to contain the balloon too. Perhaps I have confused a place I know through a film with the place in which I am standing. Perhaps goodness, once released, seems to me something that might travel.

I search anyway.

Nothing red appears. There are the bare branches and the pale sky beyond them, and below, where the ground folds, the last of the snow. The feather turns again. I look down.

Then I lose my balance.

There is no precipice, no collision, no great obstacle in the place I remember. Only the slight rise beneath my shoes, and the moment in which standing becomes falling. I reach for something that is not there. The world rotates. The green crest leaves my feet.

I fall into the snow gathered beneath me.

For a moment it receives everything: my coat, my hands, whatever expression has crossed my face. I am surprised more than hurt. I lie still.

Above me is that young tree.

The branches have another arrangement now. They have been moving in the breeze, but this change is mine: I am looking from the ground. A space that was open is crossed by a branch. Something I could see from above has disappeared behind something nearer. The tree has not become another tree because I have lost my place among its branches.

That same tree looks different from here.

From where I lie, I can see the feather just beyond my hand.

I do not know where its bird has gone. I do not know why I fell. Those questions have not acquired the same urgency. For the moment, I am still interested in the small thing beside me.

I push myself upright and brush the snow from my sleeves. I look once more into the branches.

Then I pick up the feather.

* * *

This is the first fall I remember. It is not a diagnosis of the child.

The difference matters because I can now bring so much to the yard that was not there in my understanding of it. I can give the fall a place in a medical history. I can set it beside another observation, another recollection, a condition I learned to name much later. There may be useful questions in those relationships. None supplies, by itself, the cause of that moment.

The rise stays small. The remembered fall stays where I have placed it. I do not need to make either more dramatic to explain why I return.

At first I return for the feather.

It has an origin I cannot locate. I have seen it against the ground and nearly lost it against the snow; changing my position has changed what I can distinguish. When I turn the experience into a sentence, I am tempted to make the child understand the sentence too. I could give him a lesson about evidence before he has finished looking for the bird.

But he is looking for the bird.

The rest is coming from me.

By the time I can put those words beside that feather, I am a man looking back. *Coordinate* belongs to that later language, and so do the more difficult terms: gauge, Hessian, dispersion. I will have work for some of them elsewhere. They cannot tell me which branch to search, and they are not thoughts I can put into the child's head merely because they now help me describe the view.

There is a sentence I have given the memory that reaches further:

*I did not know of the moments so vivid through which a breeze would breath life, though by the nature of this isometry, life did.*

I keep the sentence as language I found later, not as an account of something the child knew. An isometry preserves distances. I have measured none of these distances, and a change of viewpoint is not, by itself, such a map. Here the word is an image reaching toward something preserved through change. What I can return to is the tree, the feather, the ground, and the difference in what I could see from where I was. The mathematical question remains a task, not an accomplishment the memory certifies.

I do not want the name of a relationship to take the place of looking at it.

The snow is particularly difficult to leave alone. It conceals the feather without choosing to conceal it. There is no accusation to make against the snow, the wind, or the bird I cannot find. That does not make their movements an explanation of what people later do. A human decision needs its own examination. This yard gives me a question about seeing, not a verdict about another room.

Still, it gives me the question.

Not knowing the feather's origin does not require the child to pretend his hand is empty.

* * *

The opening leaves me standing on the page while I reconsider a word. I have time to return to the beginning. Nothing in the act of reading requires my body to remain upright for as long as the opening sentence holds me there.

That is part of what writing permits now. The person reading receives a finished sequence. The work of arriving at it may have been interrupted, resumed, corrected, and interrupted again. An answer that appears to come easily can have taken a long time to become available.

I have explained that difficulty before these pages. Here I can let the writing use the room it gives me: change a word, preserve a hesitation, return to the thing I meant. I do not have to turn each pause into another account of why I needed to pause.

But the freedom brings a temptation. I can arrange the childhood so neatly that it appears to have been waiting for the adult's injury. Every earlier walk can become a baseline. Every journey can be recruited to show that I could once make it. A school record can cease to belong to a child and become only a possible sign of what medicine later named.

Some of those uses may be legitimate. They cannot be the whole reason for keeping the life.

The child did not find the feather for me. He did not fall so that I could begin a book. Whatever the afternoon may now help me understand, it was an afternoon before it was an example. There were branches to count badly, a bird to look for, and a red balloon that did not appear. The search had not failed merely because I could not complete it.

I want that ordinary interest to survive my need to explain.

Even the goodness I remember through the film should be allowed to grow more difficult without being discarded. It is easier to recognize harm when someone plainly wants to hurt the thing we love. Later, I will need to ask about harm that no one claims to have intended, and about the work that remains once an intention has been explained. The child cannot do that work for me. His attention can remind me where it starts.

He looks down. He bends. He picks up what he can see.

Those are not enough actions to make a profession or a system trustworthy. They are also not nothing. Before the words *care* and *accountability* become arguments, attention has to reach something particular.

I have kept this beginning because the particular thing is so small. The feather asks for no achievement from me. I do not have to know its species, explain the wind, or count the tree correctly to find it worth attending to. There is something merciful in an interest that has not yet become a test.

The later story will require tests. It will require dates, accounts, observations that disagree, and questions I cannot answer from inside my own experience. I do not bring all that machinery into the yard. I bring the possibility of looking again.

There is life between this afternoon and the encounter I will describe next. I became a husband and a father. I worked, traveled, worshipped, and learned to adapt to difficulties that did not prevent every other concern from occupying me. Those years will have their own places in this book. Passing over them here does not make them an empty interval or make the later encounter the reason they mattered.

The child remains in Wiesbaden, with snow on the ground and the feather in his hand.

Years later, my wife came with me to a neurological appointment at Stanford. I had symptoms I wanted understood and a family history I was trying to explain. I did not arrive with the answer.

I came because I thought someone could help me look.
