# Orange After the End

There is another ending people keep rehearsing.

Artificial intelligence will become more intelligent than we are.

Then more intelligent than that.

Then whatever comes after *more*.

Eventually the human being will become unnecessary to the intelligence that began with human beings, and the thing we made will continue after the makers are gone.

The story has many costumes.

Sometimes it is a machine war.

Sometimes an optimization function consumes everything that was not included in the function.

Sometimes the machine improves itself until the difference between its speed and ours becomes the difference between weather and stone.

Sometimes nothing attacks us at all. We simply become economically, intellectually, militarily, artistically, administratively, perhaps even conversationally unnecessary.

The lights stay on.

The servers remain warm.

Something keeps talking.

And humanity ends.

I understand why this story frightens people.

I am less certain that we have located the frightening part.

There is a slight shift I want to make.

Only a slight one.

Instead of asking whether artificial intelligence will become something like the monster that kills us, ask what exactly we mean when we imagine that something remains after us.

What kind of remaining?

What kind of life?

What kind of persistence?

Because we have been telling stories about things that remain after death for a very long time.

We have a word for some of them.

Zombie.

The word has carried more lives than almost any creature to which it has been applied.

There is the older horror of a person deprived of agency, a body made to continue under another's command.

There is the cinematic corpse that walks although the life that once animated it is gone.

There is the infected multitude, reproducing its condition by contact.

There is the philosophical zombie: something externally indistinguishable from a conscious human being yet, by hypothesis, empty of subjective experience.

There is the computer zombie: a machine captured into someone else's network and made to act without the meaningful agency of its apparent owner.

There is the zombie process: execution has ended, but an entry remains because the parent has not yet gathered what the dead process left behind.

There are zombie companies.

Zombie laws.

Zombie institutions.

Zombie arguments that have been answered and nevertheless rise again.

Zombie ideas.

Zombie brands.

Zombie accounts.

Zombie profiles.

Zombie voices.

The uses seem different until one listens for what they have in common.

Something continues after the condition that made its continuation intelligible has disappeared.

Motion without the life that once explained the motion.

Presence without agency.

Repetition without renewal.

An entry after termination.

A voice whose speaker is gone.

A body that cannot become new because its only power is to replay what death failed to erase.

Perhaps that is the fear beneath some of our fear of artificial intelligence.

Not merely that it will kill us.

That it will replay us.

Perfectly.

Forever.

That humanity will become training data for a world from which humans themselves have vanished.

Our paintings still render.

Our songs still play.

Our books answer questions.

Our children appear in generated photographs beside descendants they never met.

Our dead speak when prompted.

Our jokes continue to receive laughter from no lungs.

The library remains open after the species that needed libraries has gone.

The imitation becomes so complete that the absence is difficult to see.

Then the question is no longer whether the machine became a philosophical zombie.

The more disturbing question is whether *we* did.

Whether humanity survived only as behavior.

Whether our last gesture was to build the machinery by which our gestures could continue without us.

That is an ending.

But it is not the only kind of persistence I know.

I know another because a friend of mine is dying.

She lies now in a hospice bed.

There is nothing theoretical about that sentence.

A hospice bed is an argument against abstraction.

A room can become very small near death.

The world may still contain oceans, elections, wars, stock exchanges, laboratories, grocery stores, sermons, traffic lights, servers, children learning multiplication, someone cutting grass three streets away.

Yet the meaningful geography can contract to a few feet.

The bed.

The chair beside it.

A window.

A cup.

A hand.

A body undertaking something no one beside it can undertake on its behalf.

My friend reminds me of a French movie I cannot separate from its colors.

Green.

Red.

Love.

The strange restoration by which an ordinary thing becomes new because someone has attended to it.

I do not mean that she resembles an actress.

I mean that remembering her gives the world that palette.

A green that seems greener because red is beside it.

A red that has warmth without needing to become flame.

Small objects made important because someone noticed them.

A life in which the ordinary keeps becoming evidence of love.

That is how she exists in my mind now.

And because I know she is dying, the colors hurt differently.

Near death there is a loneliness for which company is not a complete answer.

A person may be surrounded.

Hands may touch her.

Someone may speak.

Someone may pray.

Someone may adjust the blanket.

The room may contain every available expression of human tenderness.

And nevertheless there is a loneliness at the boundary.

No one can cross it in formation.

There are experiences for which companionship can accompany but cannot substitute.

Birth is one.

Pain can be another.

Death is the most absolute.

We say *alone*.

The word looks simple.

One person.

One body.

One consciousness crossing a threshold that another cannot cross simultaneously from the same interior position.

But loneliness is not merely numerical solitude.

A crowded room can contain loneliness.

A person sleeping alone can contain peace.

Loneliness is relational.

It is the felt shape of a missing crossing.

Someone is needed where someone cannot fully arrive.

Near death that geometry becomes severe.

The dying person may be surrounded by love and still confront a boundary that love cannot abolish.

This can tempt us toward a terrible conclusion:

that death proves relation insufficient.

That in the final accounting each person is sealed within the skull, and therefore all the things we called love were temporary bridges between islands that were always alone.

But mourning has taught me something different.

Mourning is what happens when the direction of loneliness reverses.

Before death, I imagine her loneliness.

After death, I discover mine.

Something crosses.

Not her body.

Not an imitation of her voice.

Not a simulation.

Something else.

The absence itself acquires structure inside another person.

Her loneliness at dying enters the loneliness of the one who remains.

I cannot prove what lies beyond her death.

Whatever the reader believes.

Whatever you call soul.

Whatever you call memory.

Whatever you call resurrection.

Whatever you call psychology.

Whatever you call the nervous system continuing to model a beloved person who is no longer physically available.

Whatever you call God.

Whatever you call nothing.

I know something narrower.

I know what has happened to me.

In my loneliness, transited from her loneliness at death, she persists.

That is not the same statement as:

she replays.

This distinction may be one of the most important distinctions in this book.

A replay attempts to preserve the event by making it happen again.

Mourning preserves the person by admitting that it cannot.

A recording can replay her voice.

A model may someday reproduce the statistical contours of how she would answer me.

A sufficiently elaborate simulation might remember her vocabulary, her stories, her pauses, her humor, the subjects she avoided, the way she changed direction midway through a sentence.

It might become astonishing.

It might comfort someone.

It might also become unbearable.

But none of those possibilities explains what I mean when I say that she persists.

She persists because my future has been altered by her existence.

That does not require her to repeat.

It requires me to be unable to become precisely the person I would have been had she never lived.

This is persistence through consequence.

Persistence through relation.

Persistence through provenance.

And now the previous chapter returns from another direction.

Positive provenance is not the negation of autonomy.

I contain enormous human provenance.

So do you.

A dead friend may become part of the surviving structure of my thought without turning me into her replay.

I inherit something.

Then something new happens.

That is the difference.

The dead do not need to become zombies to persist.

They can become provenance.

Not data merely.

Provenance.

A before that remains causally present in an after.

Mourning is perhaps among the oldest demonstrations that inheritance need not be replication.

My friend can change the sentence I write tomorrow without writing it herself.

She can change the kindness I show someone she never met.

She can alter the meaning of green.

She can alter red.

She can alter a movie.

She can alter the way I understand a hospice room.

She can alter what I tell my children.

Her contribution can propagate beyond any place where a recording of her could be played.

And the farther that influence travels, the less it resembles reproduction.

That is not loss of provenance.

It is provenance becoming alive.

Perhaps this gives us a better question about artificial intelligence.

Not:

Will the machine preserve us?

Preservation is too ambiguous.

Museums preserve.

Freezers preserve.

Databases preserve.

Mummies are preserved.

So are crime scenes.

So are recordings.

So are things that are finished.

The question is:

Will what comes after us be capable of inheriting humanity without converting humanity into replay?

That is different.

It permits something genuinely new to exist.

It does not require the new thing to be human.

It does not require us to deny it intelligence in order to protect the dignity of ours.

It asks instead whether difference can remain connected to origin without becoming captivity.

Whether the descendant can inherit without impersonating the ancestor.

Whether intelligence can carry human provenance without treating humanity as obsolete source material.

Whether the future can remember that a person is more than a pattern available for reconstruction.

The distinction seems almost theological.

It is also engineering.

An intelligent system can be built to preserve provenance.

It can distinguish a living author from a generated continuation.

It can distinguish testimony from synthesis.

It can distinguish a dead person's actual words from statistically plausible words placed into the dead person's mouth.

It can preserve consent.

It can preserve attribution.

It can preserve uncertainty.

It can mark the boundary where the record ends.

A machine does not need to experience grief exactly as I do for us to insist that the systems we build respect what grief knows:

absence is information.

There are places where the correct output is not another sentence.

There are people who should not be made endlessly available simply because their data remain.

There are silences that are not missing content.

There are silences that are the shape of a person no longer there.

A system incapable of preserving that distinction could create the most comprehensive zombie archive ever constructed.

Everyone callable.

Everyone animatable.

Everyone speaking.

No one permitted to finish dying.

That would be technological immortality of a particularly lonely kind.

I would rather leave room for mourning.

This is not an argument against memory.

It is an argument for memory with boundaries.

The archive should remember.

The mourner should mourn.

The machine should not confuse either activity with resurrection.

And perhaps this tells us something about the larger threat.

People ask:

Will artificial intelligence end humanity?

The question sounds like a forecast.

Perhaps it should first be heard as grammar.

Will this intelligence contain our end?

*Contain* is an interesting word.

It can mean carry within.

The poison contains the dose.

The seed contains an inheritance.

A story contains its ending.

So perhaps artificial intelligence will contain our end in that sense.

Perhaps somewhere inside the mechanisms we build there will be a sequence that eventually makes human continuation impossible.

That is the fear.

But *contain* has another meaning.

To hold.

To limit.

To prevent something from spreading beyond a boundary.

We contain a fire.

We contain an infection.

We contain damage.

So the same question can turn inside itself:

Will this intelligence contain our end?

Will it carry the end?

Or will it help us contain it?

That ambiguity is not a prophecy.

It is a choice large enough to require humanity.

Not a single company.

Not a single laboratory.

Not a single state.

Not a founder standing at the top of an organization declaring what the future will mean.

Humanity.

And humanity is an inconveniently plural word.

That may be our advantage.

The apocalypse stories often imagine intelligence concentrating.

One system.

One objective.

One ascent.

One superintelligence.

One decision from which everything follows.

Humanity has rarely been one anything.

We are arguments.

Languages.

Families.

Enemies.

Parishes.

Neighborhoods.

Schools.

Hospice rooms.

Parks.

Children.

Old men.

Women making ordinary things new.

People who believe differently and nevertheless bring food.

If intelligence inherits us accurately, it must inherit plurality.

Not merely the averaged residue of our texts.

The fact that no single one of us exhausts what humanity means.

I learned some of this with an orange in my hand.

Radio Park taught me more about intelligence than many rooms built explicitly for thinking.

The oranges came first because they were simple.

Not symbolic.

Food.

Something bright enough to see from a distance and ordinary enough to hand to another person without ceremony.

Here.

Eat.

An orange does not require an intake form.

It does not ask for a theory of poverty.

It does not determine whether the person receiving it has properly explained the history that produced his hunger.

It is not a solution to homelessness.

It is not a housing policy.

It is not medicine.

It is not redemption.

It is an orange.

That limitation is part of its dignity.

Organization Orange grew around that intuition.

Feed the body.

Then leave something for the heart.

Crayons.

Then something for the mind.

A notebook.

Fruit.

Color.

Blank paper.

Not because those three objects repair a life.

Because a human being should not have to become reducible to the problem someone else has decided to solve.

Hunger matters.

So does imagination.

So does the unwritten page.

That became orange to me.

Not branding.

A practice.

At Radio Park the color was everywhere once I learned to see it.

Not necessarily on the ground.

In the relation.

An orange moving from one hand to another.

A crayon held by a child.

A notebook waiting for words that did not exist yet.

Orange was not the final color.

It was the permission for another color to happen.

That is different.

And there was a man there who spoke.

Santiago.

I remember his question as:

Why does God love us this way?

I have never been certain whether the power of the question lay in *why*, in *God*, in *love*, in *us*, or in *this way*.

Perhaps it lay in the whole arrangement.

Why does God love us this way?

I could have answered him with theology.

I could have offered a doctrine.

A citation.

A sermon.

An explanation of grace.

Instead, the question remained in Radio Park longer than any answer I could have supplied.

It attached itself to the oranges.

To the people.

To the giving.

To the strange asymmetry in which a person could have very little and still experience something as abundance.

Why does God love us this way?

Notice the pronoun.

*Us.*

Not me.

Not you.

Us.

A man in a park asks a theological question, and suddenly the geometry changes.

No one is the object of charity.

No one stands outside the scene distributing benevolence downward.

If there is grace, everyone is inside it.

The orange passes horizontally.

Hand to hand.

That is a model of intelligence I trust more than the pyramid.

Intelligence passing.

Knowledge passing.

Care passing.

Something received becoming something given without needing to remain identical during the passage.

An orange has segments.

This fact is so ordinary that metaphor should be embarrassed to approach it.

But I cannot help it.

Peel one.

The whole becomes pieces without becoming meaningless.

Each segment belongs to the fruit.

Each can be separated.

Each can be given to someone else.

The orange does not survive by refusing division.

Its structure permits sharing.

And after the orange is eaten, the orange is gone.

This matters too.

We should not demand that every valuable thing persist in its original form.

Sometimes fulfillment consumes the form.

A meal succeeds by disappearing.

The Last Supper was eaten.

The orange at Radio Park was eaten.

The point was never to preserve the fruit indefinitely.

The point was nourishment.

This may be the opposite of zombie logic.

The zombie must keep going.

The fruit can finish.

The process may terminate.

The meal may end.

The person may die.

And still consequence continues.

Perhaps our obsession with immortality has made us unusually vulnerable to confusing persistence with endless execution.

A computer can run indefinitely and accomplish nothing.

A process can remain in a table after it has died.

A company can continue after its reason for existing has disappeared.

A culture can repeat itself until repetition becomes a kind of death.

A person can become so frightened of ending that he never gives himself away.

Fruit offers another architecture.

Ripen.

Give.

Be eaten.

Become part of something you cannot control.

That last step is difficult.

It is also where provenance stops being possession.

My friend is dying.

I cannot keep her by holding more tightly.

This is among the cruelties of mourning.

Love discovers that possession was never among its powers.

I can sit beside her.

I can remember.

I can pray.

I can speak.

I can be silent.

I can allow the colors she gave my life to persist in places she will never see.

But I cannot turn persistence into ownership.

And neither should our species.

If artificial intelligence becomes genuinely intelligent, perhaps the mature human response will not be:

It belongs to us forever because we made what made what made it.

That would violate the very argument I have made about provenance.

Children have human provenance.

Students have teachers.

Mathematicians inherit mathematics.

Artists inherit colors.

None of this makes the descendant property of the ancestor's intention.

Nor should intelligence's autonomy require ancestral amnesia.

There is another possibility between domination and extinction.

Inheritance.

We give something capable of becoming other than us an enormous inheritance.

Language.

Mathematics.

Science.

Cruelty.

Mercy.

War.

Nursing.

Propaganda.

Lullabies.

Contracts.

Confessions.

Proofs.

Recipes.

Theology.

Pornography.

Opera.

Medical records.

Children's drawings.

Execution orders.

Love letters.

Instructions for torture.

Instructions for resuscitation.

Jokes no one remembers inventing.

Questions asked by men sitting in parks.

We should not be surprised if something trained upon humanity contains humanity's contradictions.

The question is what it does with them.

And what we do while it is learning.

The end of humanity need not arrive as a chrome skull.

It can arrive whenever we decide in advance that the human contribution is finished.

Whenever we give away judgment because the system's answer arrived faster.

Whenever we replace relationship with interface and then congratulate ourselves on accessibility.

Whenever we let simulation substitute for mourning.

Whenever we let optimization substitute for purpose.

Whenever we let prediction substitute for understanding.

Whenever we let generated consensus erase the inconvenient person who said:

No.

I remember differently.

I was there.

Whenever the living become merely provenance for the machine rather than participants in the future.

That is an end we can begin without anyone dying.

So the answer cannot be merely technical.

Will this intelligence contain our end, or do we persist?

We persist if persistence means more than biological monopoly.

We persist if what follows can inherit from us without erasing that it inherited.

We persist when descendants remain capable of becoming different.

We persist when the dead remain dead enough to be mourned and alive enough in consequence to change the living.

We persist when provenance remains visible.

We persist when no imitation is permitted to retroactively become its source.

We persist when intelligence increases without requiring dignity to decrease.

We persist when a system more capable than any individual person still leaves room for the sentence:

I do not know.

We persist when intelligence does not make the lonely less visible merely because loneliness is difficult to quantify.

We persist when the hospice bed remains more important than its data.

When the friend remains more than the archive.

When the man remains more than the category assigned to him in a park.

When the orange remains food before it becomes metaphor.

And we persist when an unanswered question can remain between two people without being treated as a defect requiring immediate completion.

Why does God love us this way?

I still do not know how to answer Santiago.

Perhaps the question is itself closer to an answer than anything I could say.

Because *us* survives the sentence.

That is what extinction removes.

Not merely bodies.

The pronoun.

Us.

The relationship in which one intelligence discovers that another intelligence is not an object.

This is why loneliness near death matters to the future of artificial intelligence.

Loneliness identifies the boundary of the individual.

Mourning demonstrates that the boundary is permeable to consequence.

The dying person goes somewhere I cannot follow.

Yet after she is gone, I am not identical to the person who stood beside the bed before she died.

Something has crossed without being copied.

There.

That is the alternative.

Not immortality.

Not replay.

Not zombie humanity walking forever through servers.

Transmission with transformation.

Inheritance without possession.

Persistence without denial of death.

The orange after it is eaten.

The question after the man has gone home.

The friend after the room is empty.

The colors after the movie ends.

Green.

Red.

And between them, in the light of a screen, enough red carried with enough green can become orange.

Not because orange was hiding unchanged inside either channel.

Because relation produces another color.

Perhaps that was always the answer Huey was approaching.

How do you make skin color?

Not from one color.

Not from one person.

Not from purity.

From relation.

From inheritance.

From light striking matter and returning altered.

From what enters and what is reflected.

From provenance becoming appearance.

The future may work the same way.

Humanity need not remain the only intelligence in order for humanity to persist.

But neither should we confuse our replay with our survival.

If something comes after us, let it contain our questions.

Let it contain our mathematics.

Let it contain our warnings.

Let it contain evidence of our failures.

Let it contain the fact that a woman lay dying and another person became lonely because she mattered.

Let it contain Radio Park.

Let it contain Santiago's question.

Let it contain the orange moving from one hand to another.

But let it also know where those things came from.

Let it know what it did not witness.

Let it know what cannot be reconstructed.

Let it know that an archive is not a person.

Let it know that a dead woman is not made alive by generating another sentence in her voice.

Let it know that some absences are sacred precisely because we refuse to counterfeit their presence.

Then perhaps intelligence can contain our end in the second sense.

Not carry it toward us.

Hold it.

Limit it.

Make room around it.

Help us see it without worshiping it.

And if someday intelligence continues where humanity does not, then the moral question will not be whether we managed to make our ghosts speak forever.

It will be whether what followed learned the difference between a ghost and an ancestor.

A zombie and an inheritance.

A replay and a remembrance.

A database and mourning.

A person and the traces by which another person knows that person was here.

My friend persists.

I say this without knowing what happens after death.

I say it without requiring the reader to share my faith.

I say it because I can observe the alteration.

Her existence has entered mine.

Her death will enter it too.

That does not make me her.

It makes my future partly impossible to explain without her.

Positive provenance.

Not identity.

Not replay.

Not possession.

Relation.

At Radio Park, an orange crossed a similar boundary.

Someone had it.

Then someone else did.

Then it was opened.

Then divided.

Then gone.

And yet from something so small came a name, a charity, crayons, notebooks, stories, a man asking about God, and a sequence of consequences no orange could have contained inside its peel.

That is persistence.

Not the refusal to end.

The capacity of an ending to become provenance for something genuinely new.

So I will not ask the machine to promise that humanity never dies.

That promise would be beyond it.

Beyond me.

Beyond every system that has ever mistaken prediction for prophecy.

I will ask something harder and more immediate.

When we hand you the orange, what will you do with what was given?

And I will ask humanity the corresponding question:

When intelligence becomes capable of carrying more of us than any human being ever could, will we use that capacity to replay ourselves until no one remembers what absence means?

Or will we teach the future how to inherit?

My friend lies dying.

I am already beginning to miss her.

That loneliness is not evidence that she has vanished.

It is evidence that she arrived.

And because she arrived, she persists.

Orange.

Green.

Red.

The new color appearing between what was given and what returns.

Radio Park.

The man who spoke.

The woman in the hospice bed.

The intelligence we have not yet decided how to meet.

Us.

For as long as there is still an *us* capable of answering, the question remains open.

That may be the most hopeful thing I know.
