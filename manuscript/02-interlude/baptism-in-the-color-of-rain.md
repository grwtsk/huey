# Baptism in the Color of Rain

For the second time, I had been asked to come and be the white man in the room.

When I lived in Chicago, I had come past college, past the poverty of those years, past the wedding and into the marriage—into that part of life when the world begins bringing more than you knew to ask for. Life brings plenty. You learn, by degrees, how much of that plenty will require you.

A woman had just given birth.

I had been called to be the *netch asheba*.

To translate, although no passage between languages was required. The mother did not need me to speak in place of her. The father beside her did not need me to explain the woman he loved. Whatever required translation belonged to the doctors.

She had bled profusely. She was still bleeding. Labor had exhausted her; blood loss had left her looking almost ghostly. She seemed lost and worried, receding from the attention of a room in which she remained urgently present.

Her child had been born. She was still in the bed.

Beside her stood the child’s father, a man I knew to be strong.

There were three doctors.

I remember the number because I still find it difficult to accommodate: three doctors with a bleeding mother and a newborn child, speaking to one another, not addressing either parent, not appearing particularly worried.

Three.

I brought no medical knowledge they lacked. I carried no credential that entitled me to overrule them. I did not establish my standing, deliver an argument, or announce myself as an advocate prepared for a contest.

My presence changed where they looked.

They noticed the *netch*. Then they turned their attention to her.

I do not claim that these doctors were racist. I claim the shape of what I saw, and that the shape was grotesque.

The woman had not begun to need care in the interval between those two looks. The father had not become more deserving of an answer. The baby had not become more newborn.

I had entered.

That was the change I could see.

And because I was the one who had entered, because I could stand there and watch attention pass through me toward the person who had needed it before I arrived, I have never been able to remember that room without also remembering how little I had brought into it.

Only myself.

Only the person they happened to recognize.

---

Before that room, before the wedding, before I had met the woman who would become my wife, there was a day shortly after August, when twelve leaves had turned and the year had begun, almost without announcement, to move toward something else.

I was walking through Irving Park.

I have been shot at. There was a free afternoon when I stood before a firing squad and afterward befriended their commander. Only a moment before, the rifles had been pointed at me. Then I was speaking with the man who commanded them, and there was still an afternoon before us.

When I remember him, the rifles remain somewhere behind him. They have not disappeared, though they are no longer the nearest thing.

The man is nearer.

And somewhere beside that afternoon is this one: the leaves beginning to turn, the streets of Irving Park, a man inside a wrecked car.

A biker was working to get him out. I came close enough to touch the door. For a moment my finger rested against it.

Then my finger slipped.

As it slipped, the biker pulled the door right off.

The movement was his. My part in it was scarcely more than a touch. The two things happened so closely together that memory keeps offering them in one motion, and I keep separating them: my finger slipping; his strength taking the door away.

He pulled the man from the wreckage.

I was standing nearby, and I saw the bone of the thigh where the knee should have been.

Around that small, impossible place, the rest of the scene grows less distinct. There is the man, and the man pulling him free, and my hand no longer touching anything.

Then there are the houses.

I ran toward them, thinking of doors, of knocking, of finding somebody home. I meant to go from one to another until someone answered.

I could run then.

The sentence is simple enough. My body takes longer with it.

---

Years later, in Fresno, California, I established primary care with a doctor whom I came to love.

I mean love by the measures my Orthodox faith has taught me to hold together: affection and respect, gratitude and trust, a sincere desire for the other person’s good. I admired her. I trusted her judgment. I entrusted her with the life I was trying to continue living.

By my understanding of what continuing care required, I was eventually abandoned.

That is a grave word. I use it for a grave absence: a disabled man, mostly bed-bound, left without a primary-care physician.

And yet, when I think of her, I do not feel abandoned.

She had a child. Her first.

I want that fact to remain a birth, not become an accusation.

Her life was changing. She changed jobs. She prepared the papers and the arrangements for transferring care. She was organized. She told my wife that one day she would be leaving.

My wife, not me.

Then she was gone.

She had not gone far. She had not disappeared from the hospital system. She remained a doctor, working in another role within the same institutional world.

But from the place where I lay, she was gone.

I no longer had access to a primary-care physician.

A considerable part of a year can pass inside the work of getting through a day. Then another part passes. I learned to work around the absence as I worked around a joint that would no longer bend, asking more of everything that still could. The arrangement became familiar. There was still no doctor.

Well over a year had passed before I spoke to my wife about the concern. She called the doctor.

The apology was immediate.

I remember its quality. An apology has a way of placing itself before the person who has been hurt. Hers seemed to carry reverence for the act itself. It did not seem to be searching for an exit.

The hospital, she explained, was supposed to help find me another doctor.

“Did you call?”

There was the distance between the arrangement and the patient.

Somewhere, a transfer had been prepared. Here, I remained without care.

In Irving Park, I had been running toward the houses, preparing to knock. But the people were already outside. Before I reached a door, there was someone to ask.

“Call nine one one,” I cried.

A woman answered, her worry softening the words.

“I am.”

I had been reaching toward the next thing that needed doing, and she was already doing it.

For a moment, her two words were enough. There was someone at the car. There was someone making the call. I did not have to find another door.

Yes.

I called.

At one end of the telephone was a woman answering for the institution. At the other was a man trying to deliver the words his mind had formed. They were words I knew. Getting them across asked something else of me, and the effort could sound drunken.

I was doing my best.

“I was abandoned by a doctor. Could you document that for me?”

She became upset.

“You are required to document that for me.”

Her anger grew.

“The law says you are required to document this.”

Those were the words I used. I was invoking what I understood to be a requirement, trying to give my request an authority my voice could no longer reliably carry.

As I understood her response, this was not her job. It belonged to medicine. She was staff.

I do not know what the institution had trained or empowered her to do with a serious word arriving in a difficult voice. I know that I had called the institution and reached her.

She was not my physician. She was the person answering through the route by which I was supposed to reach care.

I needed my report received as my report and passed to someone who could respond. I was asking her to pass on an absence of care I was reporting, not to decide whether abandonment had occurred or settle the law. I needed my words to arrive somewhere they could be answered.

In Chicago, I had entered a room and attention had followed me toward a woman who was bleeding.

Now I was trying to enter through a telephone.

The person was still here. The words were taking longer.

Instead of reaching medicine, I had reached the place where medicine said it was somewhere else.

Then I felt abandoned.

---

After the woman in Irving Park answered me, I continued my walk.

The houses passed beside me again, although I had not quite returned to the walk I had been taking. My finger had slipped from the door; the door had come away. Those movements remained close, even as the distance from the car grew longer.

I had reached the edge of what I could do and found other people doing what they could.

There had been somewhere to put the next moment.

Years later, at Saint Agnes, I went to a pain clinic.

I arrived in my wheelchair, moving with the laborious unsteadiness that can look like intoxication to someone who does not know what it costs me to move. I wore sunglasses because of the light sensitivity that accompanies my genetic condition.

A nurse looked at me.

She scowled.

I make allowances for people. Even now, I begin reaching for them: perhaps she did not know; perhaps she was tired; perhaps I misunderstood her expression.

The degeneration inside my head is not visible to someone looking at my face. What becomes visible is the difficulty of getting a word or a movement out of it. In the blunt language I sometimes use for what is happening, my brain rots.

I do not know whether she had read my record.

Later, the nurse entered the small examination room with the doctor. My wife was beside me.

The doctor insisted on a drug test. Just a quick one, so he could be sure.

I cannot tell you whether the look and the test were connected. I can tell you what had brought me there.

Pain.

I had been in pain for more than two decades. Several conditions, not one, had become entangled in the life I was trying to live.

I received no relief that day.

I am still in pain.

As I type this, a fuzzy fire extends from behind my eyes toward my toes. It gathers around the joints. Movement makes it sharper. There is something woolly about its edges, something indistinct, until I try to bend and discover how precisely it can answer.

I stop to rub my wrist. I stretch my hand. I return to the sentence.

When I could stand and sit more freely, I changed between them many times a minute, looking for a position in which my body might briefly stop insisting on itself.

My fingers are always stiff.

I have had to learn to type again. Not by touch, but by *minimum touch*: the smallest movement that will deliver the letter, the least expenditure that will bring the word into being.

The keys themselves do not hurt me. The accumulating stiffness does.

A sentence has a physical cost that is not visible in the sentence.

You can read this much faster than I can make it. By the time you reach the end of a paragraph, the pauses in which I rubbed my wrist have disappeared. The words draw together, carrying you past the places where I had to stop. You do not have to wait for my hand.

It is the hand that touched the car door.

Scarcely more than a touch then, too.

The biker pulled the door away. I did not. These fingers deliver the words now, and the smallness of their movement tells you little of what it asks of me.

There is also something I can only call pain memory.

At first, a pain arrives as an event. Later, it can arrive carrying the others.

Take a kidney stone. On the scale I have been asked to use, I might place one at a gentle five. Other people describe that pain as legendary. I do not doubt them. I am describing the ruler I have been left with, not correcting theirs.

The instruction, as it has been given to me, is to count from one to ten, with ten being the worst pain of my life, and say where I am now.

But what happens when the worst pain of your life keeps acquiring a history?

What is worse than a single stone?

The memory of a hundred.

That is what I call ten: the present pain with the old pain gathering around it. My body recognizes what may be beginning before I am ready to endure it again. The scale asks where I am now; my answer has a history.

As I write, I am at three.

In the comparisons available to me, it is something like a devastating arthritic ache.

Three is the number under which I am writing this sentence.

The number does not show you my hand.

---

In time, the ambulance passed me with the man inside.

I was still walking through Irving Park.

Its lights were flashing. Its siren was sounding. I watched it go along the street, carrying him farther than I could follow, while I went on at the pace of someone walking.

Then the siren stopped.

For a little while the lights continued without it, and I watched them in the quiet.

Then those went out as well.

I did not know what had happened inside.

From where I was, there had been sound and light, and then there was an ambulance farther down the street.

I cannot write his ending from the street.

I can tell you where my finger was. I can tell you who pulled the door away. I can tell you what the woman said and the order in which the signals ceased.

The man himself had passed beyond what I could see.

---

There are also things I know about myself that have become wrong in someone else’s account.

I was misgendered by a doctor.

More than once.

I had a beard. I had the body, altered by illness, in which I had always known myself as a man. More simply, I was a man. That had not become uncertain to me.

Whatever force I once carried in a glare had diminished. My body no longer assembled itself into an easy picture of vitality and controlled strength. I had never been particularly muscular. I was long, slender, boyish. I walked differently. I spoke differently.

I had been called gay before.

In the Protestant world I knew, an untrained eye could gather those features into a verdict and feel no further need to inquire.

But a gay man is not thereby a woman. A woman is not what a man becomes by failing somebody else’s examination of masculinity.

Nor is *woman* an insult.

The wrongness was in the failure to describe the person whose care was at issue.

Not some passing donkey in the street getting me wrong.

A doctor writing about me.

When I first began to go through this disease—when I mean *really* began to go through it, when it could no longer be managed around the edges of an otherwise ordinary day—I had a thought that needs to be held carefully.

I did not want to become like my mother.

I meant the disease we share. I meant what I had watched her endure.

The rest of her is fabulous.

I did not fear becoming like the person I loved. I feared suffering as I had seen her suffer.

Even now, she comes to me first through a word more intimate than a category.

*Mom.*

That is the nearest name I have for her. It tells you how I know her, not everything she is.

And where the larger word—*Woman*—gives her power, that power belongs to her. It takes nothing from my being a man.

As my own body became harder to inhabit, another thought began. I could not quite finish it.

*Transitions, when your mind is so aware of your body…*

My illness did not tell me what another person’s transition was. It brought me to the edge of a life I had not lived, where explanation would have required more knowledge than I possessed.

What I found there was courage.

To the transgender people among my neighbors, my teachers, my friends, and those whose paths I cross: I see you. I love you.

In my heart, imagine a thousand stadia, each filled with symphony and applause.

Let the strings rise. Let the brass answer. Let the answering become so immense that no one has to lean toward my mouth to discover whether the welcome was meant for them. It was. It is. Let there be room for the person at the farthest edge to hear it without first coming forward to explain.

There is a welcome larger than the voice I can give it.

Let the music carry that.

A Sikh man who had not cut his hair since birth and I might find principles upon which to build an understanding. We might speak of the body, of faithfulness, of what it means to keep a sanctuary.

But I would leave that building in a breath to meet you where you stood.

I would not bring its walls.

I remain beneath Christ Pantocrator. My place beneath Him does not give me the right to place you beyond His love.

He loves you.

I am a man.

I have done things. Yes! There are things I have not done. There is a penis. That is a fact about my body, not a law I am composing for yours.

And I have been misgendered in my medical record exactly twice.

I do not know how those words found their way into the record. I know they were meant to describe me, and they were wrong.

The first time, I looked down at my hand.

There it was.

Mine.

Sometimes, in its stiffness, in the familiar difficulty of persuading a finger to do a little thing, I find something of my mother there.

I have looked more than once. Looking, at least, does not ask the fingers to bend.

The resemblance does not confuse me. It brings me near her.

I am still her son, recognizing in my hand a little of what she has endured.

---

These hands have done things.

“I saved a child from drowning,” I like to say.

But that is not the whole truth. I have saved children. Many.

The little *ren* at the end of *children* is harder for my dysarthria to deliver. A plural can ask more of my mouth than the memory asks of my mind.

So I begin where my speech will let me.

I saved a child from drowning.

His mother jumped in too. I reached him first: a leap, then two and one-over-two-pi strokes.

That strange arithmetic is how I remember the movement. Two strokes, and then the fraction my body keeps. Not quite another stroke. Not yet close enough without it.

I lifted him.

His eyes were open. I could not see him breathe.

Then I placed him in his mother’s arms.

She was cold from the water. The warmth I remember was in the way she held him.

I had reached him first. She had come for him too.

The child passed from my hands into her embrace.

I knew where I had placed him.

My hands, which now have to negotiate with a keyboard, once did that. And they are also the hands of the man who stood near the wreckage while somebody else pulled a stranger free.

Follow either memory and you come back to these fingers.

In one, they lift.

In another, a finger slips.

Here, they ask for the smallest movement that will carry the next word.

I do not need them to have saved a child to ask that someone attend to them now.

But I want their life in the telling. I want the child in his mother’s arms and the biker with the door and the woman already making the call. I want the hand’s resemblance to my mother to remain beside what the hand has done, without any of those things being required to cancel another.

When I look at the commander, the rifles are still behind him.

When I think of the doctor I loved, her first child remains a birth.

When I remember the bleeding mother, there are still three doctors in the room.

And when I go back to Irving Park, I still do not know what happened inside the ambulance.

The woman’s answer has not changed.

“I am.”

---

When I lived in Chicago, I met a man named Kannan.

When I lived in Salt Lake—or when memory takes me there now—I meet him as Jordon.

Father John baptized me.

I was once Bobby. Then I met a Robert and took that name. My father often called me Rob.

My middle name has always been Arthur.

*Like the king,* I sometimes think, allowing myself that small, private coronation.

A life can carry several names without becoming several strangers. I know the voices that belong to mine. I know how my father’s shortening of a name can still contain the whole person he was calling.

Then came Rahel.

And somewhere in the middle, we became R.A.

Letters I had carried alone came to hold a *we*.

My wife is beside me in so many of the rooms I have told you about that I have to stop myself from placing her in all of them. Before she was beside my wheelchair, before she called the doctor, before the wedding and the marriage, there was a young woman whom I had not yet learned to call my wife.

And before I met her, twelve leaves had turned.

The man in the ambulance was already beyond my knowing. The woman by the houses had already answered. There had been an afternoon in which I spoke with the commander whose rifles had been pointed at me.

All of that belonged to a life in which I had not yet seen the thing in her hair.

I must go back now.

Chicago.

The building on Wabash.

Room 232.

She wore a thing in her hair.

I know what it is called now. But that knowledge does not get to walk backward into the room and improve the young man who saw her. In that moment, it was a thing. It remains the thing in her hair by which she first entered my heart.

It was sky blue, with a preponderance of light that seemed made of metal.

The colors touched without settling into one another. Ink-black held a white-silver gleam. Pale green flashed toward platinum. Light caught among the threads so finely that I could not tell where color ended and reflection began.

At the edges, the fibers lifted into white.

Little beginnings of fray.

Even what might come undone was luminous.

Green sparkled over the arch of her cheek. I remember the curve and the way the color lay along it. Here, the old effort to name what I was seeing gives way. I can tell you sky blue, pale green, platinum. I can bring the colors close together on the page.

She was there.

I looked down.

She wore maroon above the Jordy and Congress blues of her Diesel jeans.

Then I noticed her breasts.

Immediately, the management of my eyes became a public responsibility.

I looked back at my screen.

I was excited. I counted moments until eighty-seven, as though a number could give the waiting somewhere to stand.

Eighty-seven.

Then she looked at me.

“What are you doing?”

Every word but the last carried its weight differently from what the white men who had instructed my ear would have required.

The question reached me whole.

Nothing in it needed to be repaired before I could understand her.

I remember the pause before I answered. All the years in which I would remember her asking had yet to happen. She had not yet stood beside me in the examination rooms. We had not yet become the two letters I would find inside my own name. I had not yet learned how much of a day might be spent persuading a hand to continue.

Nor did any of that have to enter the room before I could answer.

For that moment, there was only her question, the light among the threads, and the answer I had not given.

The pause before love had a name.

“I am making rain,” I said, humbly.

---

When I lived in Chicago, there was a man who preached on the train.

I remember the rattle beneath his voice, the crackle around it, the low brown wash of machinery through which he spoke. Memory gives his voice the full, fat color of jazz.

Then the announcement came in, as though he had raised a hand to cue it.

“This is Grand.”

*My station,* I thought, for a moment.
