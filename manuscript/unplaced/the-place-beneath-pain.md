## The Place Beneath Pain

There is another perspective.

This story is old. This story is very old. Long before there were hospitals, diagnostic codes, ethics committees, disability forms, appellate courts, neurological examinations, or machines that could look through the body, there was suffering.

We all know this story.

I have carried an idea for some time that I have been reluctant to put plainly into words. Some ideas become less accurate when spoken too quickly. They invite a comparison that was never intended, or they sound like a claim to holiness when what one meant was only recognition. So I will not begin with the claim. I will walk around it.

There is a story about a Man who suffered because of the sins of others, and for the sins of the world.

I use the word *sin* deliberately. Crime is too narrow. Crime belongs to a jurisdiction. It requires elements, evidence, perhaps intent, perhaps a statute, perhaps a court competent to say what happened and what consequence follows. Sin reaches somewhere law cannot. Sin includes what can be perfectly legal and still be a failure of love. It includes indifference. Betrayal. Cowardice. Pride. The turning of one person into an object. The refusal to see. The refusal to hear. The moment when another person's suffering becomes administratively inconvenient and is therefore translated into something easier to manage.

Christianity does not begin its account of suffering by pretending that suffering is good. Orthodoxy is more careful than that. Illness, suffering, and death belong to the brokenness of the world, not to some divine appetite for pain. Orthodox teaching explicitly rejects the idea that God delights in suffering; its account of the Cross is not that the Father needed to hurt the Son in order to be satisfied. Christ freely enters the condition into which humanity has fallen and, in the Orthodox proclamation, defeats death from inside death. ([oca.org](https://www.oca.org/orthodoxy/the-orthodox-faith/doctrine-scripture/the-symbol-of-faith/redemption?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That distinction matters to me.

If suffering were simply good, then medicine would become suspicious. An analgesic might interfere with sanctification. A surgeon might interrupt a lesson God intended to teach. A doctor could watch pain deepen and call it spiritual formation.

That is not Christianity.

The Orthodox medical tradition says almost the opposite. Human healing is understood as *synergy*: human beings cooperating with God's purposes through knowledge, medicine, nourishment, surgery, care, prayer, and presence. Its bioethical tradition insists that the patient possesses an inviolate dignity as the image and likeness of God and is never merely a thing upon which technique is performed. It explicitly rejects the deliberate infliction of unnecessary suffering. Saint Basil's fourth-century ministry to the sick is remembered not because he developed a theology explaining why the diseased should remain diseased, but because Christian love built a place to care for them. ([goarch.org](https://www.goarch.org/-/for-the-health-of-body-and-soul-an-eastern-orthodox-introduction-to-bioethics?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So when I ask what suffering means, I am not asking permission for anyone to cause more of it.

I am asking what happens after it is already here.

That is a different question.

There are many medical answers to why human beings suffer. They stretch from the instant of an injured nerve backward through evolution: nociception protects tissue; fear changes behavior; memory teaches avoidance; stress reallocates attention; an organism that could not recognize danger would not survive long enough to reproduce. Even pain itself is not a single thing. Under extreme stress the nervous system can, in some circumstances, transiently suppress pain through descending inhibitory systems involving endogenous opioids and other mechanisms. The phenomenon is sufficiently well established to have a name: stress-induced analgesia. It is not universal, and stress can also increase pain. Biology is not obliged to be philosophically tidy. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30314566/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

But pain and suffering are not synonyms.

Eric Cassell made this distinction central to modern medical ethics. Pain occurs in bodies; suffering happens to persons. A person suffers when something threatens the integrity of the self—the body, certainly, but also one's relationships, purposes, history, agency, place in the world, imagined future, and capacity to remain recognizably oneself. Cassell's warning was severe: medicine can be technically competent and nevertheless become a source of suffering when it treats disease while failing to apprehend the person who has the disease. ([pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7057823/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This comes closer to what I mean.

I have wondered whether suffering has volume. Whether there is some quantity of it beyond which the vessel is full.

Is suffering itself infinite?

Do human beings possess an unlimited capacity to suffer?

Here I can answer with unusual authority, though only the authority one has over the boundaries of one's own flesh:

No.

We are creatures.

We are finite.

Something eventually gives.

Sometimes consciousness changes. Sometimes the nervous system suppresses one signal so that another imperative can dominate. Sometimes the mind narrows itself around the next survivable second. Sometimes a person collapses. Sometimes an organism can go no farther.

And eventually there is death.

There is nothing romantic in that.

But I have encountered another boundary before death, and it is stranger.

There is a point at which suffering can become partially detached from pain. I do not mean that pain disappears. I do not mean that injury ceases to matter. I mean that pain can lose its position as the governing fact.

Something else takes command.

At first I thought the proper sentence was: *the suffering becomes a preserving force.*

I would now make it more exact.

Suffering is not what preserves me.

Suffering applies the pressure.

Under enough pressure, what preserves me becomes visible.

Love.

Duty.

Memory.

Witness.

Truth.

Prayer.

My wife.

My children.

The obligation not to hand another human being the same suffering merely because it reached me first.

The insistence that whatever has happened to the body does not acquire jurisdiction over the soul.

Perhaps what I had mistaken for the preserving power of suffering was the discovery that pain is not sovereign.

This is very close to the Christian virtue called *hypomonē*: endurance, steadfastness, remaining under the weight without becoming identical to the weight. Orthodoxy does not tell the Christian to manufacture suffering. One Orthodox reflection puts the distinction plainly: suffering may be borne and transformed, but it should not be sought or invented. The Cross is not a command to become one's own executioner. ([oca.org](https://www.oca.org/cdn/PDFs/pastorallife/ptp-2.1-1991.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That correction matters.

It matters particularly in medicine.

A physician must never hear a patient say, *I have found meaning in my suffering*, and translate that into, *therefore his suffering need not be relieved.*

The meaning belongs to the sufferer.

The obligation belongs to the physician.

There is a useful philosophical asymmetry here. Emmanuel Levinas's thought has been interpreted as a warning against making someone else's suffering useful by explaining why it was necessary. My suffering may become meaningful to me. I may make from it testimony, fidelity, art, prayer, moral responsibility, even love. But another person does not acquire the right to justify my suffering because I managed to survive it. The ethical response to another's suffering remains aid, attention, compassion, medicine, and an attempt to alleviate what can be alleviated. ([mdpi.com](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/9/4/114?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That may be one of the cleanest boundaries between theology and cruelty.

I may carry my cross.

You may help me carry it.

You may not nail me to it and then congratulate me on my spiritual growth.

Medicine, at its best, understands this instinctively. The American Medical Association describes the patient-physician relationship as a moral activity arising from the imperative to care for patients and alleviate suffering. Its ethics of palliative care extends that duty beyond physical symptoms to psychological, social, spiritual, intellectual, and existential distress. Trust, dignity, communication, continuity, autonomy, and relief of suffering are not ornaments around medicine. They are among the things that make medicine medicine. ([code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/patient-physician-relationships?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Which brings me back to a question I ask more often than I admit.

*What would Jesus do?*

I do not ask it because I saw the letters printed on a bracelet.

I ask because it is a genuine test of purity.

It is an inconvenient question precisely because the answer is rarely, *whatever makes me safest*.

But neither is the answer, *whatever hurts most*.

Christ sometimes withdrew from those who intended to kill Him. He passed out of hostile crowds. He did not confuse needless exposure with courage. Withdrawal itself is therefore not cowardice. Refusing an unsafe path is not necessarily refusing the destination.

But He did not surrender His vocation.

When the hour came, He went toward Jerusalem.

When His friends were on the water, He did not make turbulence into a theological abstraction from the shore.

He went to them.

The point of walking upon the water was not that danger is holy.

The point was that someone He loved was on the other side of it.

That distinction has become important to me.

There were things from which I wanted to protect my children simply because I had learned what the world could do. One of my sons chose to attend and then graduated from a Stanford high school despite my misgivings.

I let him.

That sentence needs care too.

I am not comparing my son's education with Golgotha. I am not comparing myself with the Father. The distances between those things are immeasurable.

But fatherhood taught me something smaller that the Gospel makes larger: love cannot turn protection into imprisonment.

My fear did not give me ownership of my son's future.

It was his choice.

Choice is that important because choice is one of the places where a human life becomes the person's own.

Even when the person choosing is still becoming a man.

Even when his father is afraid.

This is also why medical ethics cannot be separated from theology quite as cleanly as modern institutions sometimes imagine. Autonomy is not merely a legal switch labeled *consent*. It is recognition that there is someone here. Someone whose body is not raw material. Someone whose fear does not cancel his intelligence. Someone whose disability does not dissolve his authority. Someone whose suffering does not make him less a person but may make the obligation to recognize his personhood more urgent.

Orthodox Christianity has an especially strong word for this because it sees the human being not merely as a rights-bearing biological organism but as an icon: the image of God.

The person before the physician is never just the disease.

Never just the chart.

Never just the difficult communication.

Never just the behavior.

Never just the body that failed.

Never just the appointment that took too long.

Never just the problem whose solution exceeds the allotted hour.

A human being is there.

This is why listening becomes something more than courtesy. It becomes an ethical act. One Orthodox reflection on compassion gives remarkably simple advice: take time for the other person; do not pass by; let the person know he has been heard. We may have no answer yet. Listening is still an action. ([oca.org](https://www.oca.org/reflections/fr.-john-jillions/june-30-2015?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

And sometimes *I do not know* is an honorable answer.

It is not the absence of medicine.

The absence of medicine begins when *I do not know* becomes *therefore I am finished with you.*

There is a great moral difference between uncertainty and abandonment.

I can forgive uncertainty.

I expect uncertainty.

The human body is sufficiently complicated to humble any honest person who studies it closely.

What frightens me is certainty without examination and departure without resolution: the person with authority deciding that the limits of his knowledge have somehow become the limits of the patient's reality.

Christianity gives me little permission to do that to another person.

The Gospel continually reverses the direction of attention. Christ sees the person everyone else has reduced to a category: blind, leprous, bleeding, possessed, sinful, foreign, dead. He does not cure every human affliction before the Crucifixion. Christianity does not offer us that sentimental story.

It offers something harder.

God does not remain at a safe distance from suffering.

He enters it.

And this brings me finally to the thought I was afraid to say.

Above the shelf under which I spend so much of my life is the icon of Christ Pantocrator. Behind it is the Cross.

Sometimes I look upward.

I see His suffering.

And I am in it.

I need to state immediately what I mean.

I do not mean that my suffering is equal to His.

I do not mean that suffering has made me righteous.

It has not.

I do not mean that I possess some private revelation unavailable to others.

I certainly do not mean that I am Christ.

I am not.

I have no pretension of being godly.

I am, indeed, a great sinner.

Forgive me.

What I mean is simpler and, to me, more terrible.

I recognize the place.

Not its magnitude.

Not its salvific power.

The place.

There is an interior geography to suffering. There is the first country of pain, where the mind protests that this cannot be happening. There is fear. Then bargaining. Then the exhausting labor of explaining oneself. Then the region where explanation begins to fail. Then the strange country where one's old measures of fairness no longer seem large enough to describe anything.

And deeper still there is a place where the question changes.

Not:

*How do I make this stop?*

But:

*Who will I be while it has not stopped?*

That question has frightened me more than pain.

Because it belongs to freedom.

Pain can be inflicted.

Freedom must still answer.

And there, when I look at the Cross, I recognize something.

Christ's victory is not that no one succeeded in hurting Him.

They did.

It is not that His body proved invulnerable.

It did not.

It is not that He escaped death.

He died.

The Christian claim is much stranger: none of those things acquired the final authority to tell Him what He would become.

Hatred could injure Him without making Him hateful.

Betrayal could wound Him without making Him betray.

Humiliation could descend upon Him without teaching Him contempt.

Violence could kill Him without making violence His answer.

Death itself could receive Him without being permitted the last word.

Orthodox Christianity places the Cross and Resurrection together so insistently for precisely this reason: Christ does not merely endure death. He passes through it and transforms its meaning. The Cross is therefore not the worship of suffering. It is the refusal to allow suffering and death to become gods. ([oca.org](https://www.oca.org/reflections/metropolitan-tikhon/sermon-on-the-feast-of-the-exaltation-of-the-cross?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Perhaps that is the place I know.

Or rather: perhaps I know one human corner of it.

At first I want to say something much more intimate:

*There is a place in suffering that perhaps only He and I share.*

But Orthodoxy corrects me here too.

The Cross is not private property.

The Cross is crowded.

His Mother is there.

John is there.

The thief is there.

The centurion eventually sees something there.

Behind them come martyrs, confessors, widows, prisoners, children, the sick, the frightened, the abandoned, those who died unnamed, those who survived and could not explain how, those who prayed beautifully, those who could no longer pray at all, and those whose only remaining prayer was to continue breathing.

There are doctors there too.

The ones who stayed.

The ones who could not cure but would not disappear.

The ones who understood that the person before them required more than technique.

The ones who relieved pain without stealing meaning.

The ones who respected refusal without withdrawing care.

The ones who knew when to speak.

The ones who knew when not to.

The ones who remained beside the bed when there was nothing left to display except fidelity.

No, the place is not shared only by Him and me.

That would make suffering another form of pride.

What I can say is this:

I know a place from inside my own body that I recognize when I look at Him.

And the Christian mystery is not that I managed somehow to climb into His suffering.

It is that He had already descended into mine.

So I return to the original question.

Is suffering infinite?

No.

I do not believe the human person has an infinite capacity to suffer. Bodies break. Minds become overwhelmed. Speech disappears. Consciousness changes. People die. Christianity should never be used to demand limitless endurance from a finite creature.

But perhaps I was asking the wrong question.

Perhaps suffering does not have to be infinite for love to exceed it.

Perhaps endurance is not the discovery that I can absorb limitless pain.

Perhaps it is the discovery that pain does not receive limitless authority.

There are things suffering can take.

There are things medicine should fight with every honorable instrument available to prevent it from taking.

And there are things that, while I remain capable of choosing, I will not give it.

My love for my children.

My obligation to truth.

My capacity to forgive without calling evil good.

My obligation to protect another person from what I have experienced.

My willingness to be corrected.

My refusal to confuse accusation with judgment.

My refusal to confuse suffering with holiness.

My refusal to let another man's failure determine what kind of man I will become.

My hope.

My faith.

My answer.

I used to think that somewhere at the bottom of suffering I had discovered suffering's deepest secret.

I think now that suffering was only what removed everything covering it.

What remained was not suffering.

What remained was the person who still had to choose.

And above me, Christ Pantocrator.

Behind Him, the Cross.

I see His suffering.

And I am in it—

not because I have entered His place,

but because He entered mine.
